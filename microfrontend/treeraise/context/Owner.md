## Post 1 — Scaling with Quality Discipline

During one of the highest-demand periods we had ever seen, I made a decision that confused a lot of people.

We slowed down.

Supply was strong. Momentum was real. Everything on paper said: go faster.

But the incoming volume was outpacing our ability to maintain the quality of shoes we shipped overseas. And quality wasn't just a metric for us — it was the livelihood of micro-entrepreneurs in markets around the world. They resold what we sent. If we shipped junk, they couldn't sell. If they couldn't sell, we had failed the mission.

So I took the team to the warehouse.

Not for a meeting. Not for a presentation. I walked them through what qualified as high-quality, gently worn footwear and what did not. I wanted them to feel the weight of the decision, not just understand it intellectually.

We processed fewer shoes that quarter.

It was the right call.

Growth is not always about volume. Sometimes the most disciplined thing a leader can do is slow the machine down to protect what the machine is actually for.

Speed is easy. Discipline is the competitive advantage.

## Post 2 — Leadership and Humanity

Years ago, a woman named Mrs. Busch taught me in high school.

Decades later, she came to visit our office.

She didn't know a single person on our team. But she sat down, asked about each of them by name, and slowly — hand by hand — wrote personal notes to people she had never met.

She had recently lost her husband. She was moving more slowly than she used to. But she kept writing.

Every note was specific. Every word was intentional. No one asked her to do it. There was no recognition waiting for her on the other side. She did it because she believed people deserved to feel seen.

I have run organizations that touched tens of millions of people. I have sat in rooms with executives who had bigger titles and larger budgets than I will ever have.

I have never seen leadership demonstrated more clearly than I did watching Mrs. Busch write those notes.

Technology will not do this for you. AI will not do this for you. A system will not do this for you.

Seeing people — truly seeing them — is still a human decision. And it still changes everything.

## Post 3 — AI and the Marketing Industry

The marketing industry is being restructured in real time and most organizations are not moving fast enough to adapt.

Forrester projects a 15% reduction in marketing jobs on top of the 8% reduction already recorded in 2025. Amazon, Accenture, Citigroup, and Intel have all announced workforce reductions tied directly to automation and AI.

This is not a distant trend. It is happening now.

Here is what I believe leaders need to understand:

Every role on your team should be able to answer one question clearly: does this directly connect to revenue growth? If the answer is no, or if the answer is uncertain, that role is at risk — not because it lacks value, but because automation is making the case against it louder every quarter.

The leaders who will define the next decade are not the ones who resist this shift. They are the ones who equip their teams to move through it.

Build AI fluency into your organization now. Not next year. Not after the next planning cycle.

Retrain before you restructure.

But do not mistake this for a purely technical challenge. The organizations that will win are still the ones built on trust, relationships, creativity, and teams that are optimized — not just automated.

AI is a tool. Leadership is still the differentiator.

## Post 4 — Leadership Through Allowing Mistakes

I watched a mistake forming in real time.

My team was making a strategic decision about where to concentrate spend in a specific market. I saw the flaw in their thinking early. I knew where it was likely to go.

I said nothing.

Not because I did not care. Because I understood something that took me years to learn: you cannot build real ownership by preventing every mistake. When you constantly intervene, you create dependence. You become the ceiling.

So I let them run the play.

They experienced the outcome firsthand. No filtered debrief. No softened summary. The consequence of the decision landed directly on the people who made it.

What happened next changed the team.

Accountability deepened. Execution sharpened. They stopped waiting for me to validate the next move.

The best leaders I have ever studied did not try to protect their teams from difficulty. They created conditions where difficulty became the teacher.

Your job is not to be right every time. Your job is to build people who can be right without you.

## Post 5 — Growth vs. Speed

A partner came to us with a real problem: demand in their market was growing and they needed faster turnaround.

On the surface, this was exactly the kind of momentum every operator wants to see.

We said no.

Not because we did not care about their growth. Because we understood what our product actually meant to the people receiving it. The reused shoes we shipped were inventory for micro-entrepreneurs. Their ability to sell depended entirely on the quality of what we sent.

If we accelerated to match demand and quality dropped, we would not be serving the mission — we would be eroding it. We would be making their business harder while making ours look more productive.

This is the tension that separates operators from just people who are busy.

Speed is seductive. Momentum feels like progress. But scaling faster than your values allow is not growth — it is risk that has not hit the income statement yet.

We held the standard. The partnership grew stronger because of it.

Protect the outcome for your partners. It is the only thing that makes the model worth scaling.

## Post 6 — Sustainability and Reuse

The most expensive solution is rarely the best one.

We were evaluating an infrastructure decision on a project and someone proposed the obvious path: new materials, standard installation, done.

Then someone else asked a different question: what is already here?

Existing fence posts set in concrete. Still structurally sound. Still functional. Just overlooked because new felt easier to plan.

We reused them.

The same thinking applied to another challenge on the same project. Rather than building over a section with running water, we explored a pallet walkway — a simple, low-cost solution that protected the ecosystem while solving the access problem.

Two decisions. Zero waste. Lower cost. Better outcomes.

I think about this in business constantly.

Before you purchase, ask what you already have. Before you build, ask what you can repurpose. Before you hire, ask whether what you need is already in the room.

Reuse is not just an environmental principle. It is an operational one.

The organizations that waste least tend to last longest.

## Post 7 — Resilience and Reflection

For most of my career, resilience meant one thing: keep moving.

Push through. Outwork the problem. Build faster than the setbacks arrive. That mindset built everything I have. I do not regret a single year of it.

Then I was diagnosed with cancer.

And for the first time, pushing harder was not an option. The body will not negotiate with the mind the way a business will. There was no workaround. There was no pivot.

There was only stopping.

What I found in that stillness surprised me. Clarity I had not had in years. Perspective on decisions I had been forcing instead of seeing clearly. A deeper understanding of what I was actually building and who I was building it for.

Real resilience, I learned, is not the ability to never stop. It is the judgment to know when stopping is the right move — and the courage to do it without guilt.

Some of the strongest leaders I know are people who have learned to pause. Not because they ran out of energy, but because they understood that clarity is a competitive advantage too.

You do not always find the answer by moving faster.

Sometimes you find it by being still.
