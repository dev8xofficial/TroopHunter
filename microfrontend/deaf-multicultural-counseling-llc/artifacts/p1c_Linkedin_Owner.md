About
Dr. Ikegami is a clinical social worker with over 15 years of experience serving the Deaf community. His expertise focuses on mental health and culturally responsive practices, grounded in his DSW research on social workers' experiences with Deaf and hard of hearing clients. He served as President of the American Deafness and Rehabilitation Association (ADARA) from 2023-2025 and teaches in Master of Social Work programs at Gallaudet University and Arizona State University.

As ADARA 2026 comes to a close, I have been reflecting on this year’s conference theme: Reaching New Peaks.

For many years, professionals across Deaf education, rehabilitation, behavioral health, interpreting, vocational rehabilitation, and related fields have worked to improve access. Access remains essential and continues to require advocacy, collaboration, and systems-level change.

Yet one question stayed with me throughout the conference:

What comes after access?

As someone who was born and raised outside the United States, I recognize that not every form of access can be granted. While I have access to opportunities and experiences within American systems, I cannot fully access the lived experiences of those who were born and raised here.

This reflection has led me to think about what may come next. If access opens the door, perhaps our work must also include awareness, acceptance, and alignment. We may not share the same experiences, but we can learn from one another, recognize different lived experiences, and align around shared goals and values.

Maybe reaching new peaks is not only about expanding access. Maybe it is also about strengthening our ability to work together across differences, recognizing that some experiences cannot be fully shared, yet meaningful collaboration remains possible.

One lesson I am taking away from this conference is that we do not have to walk the same path to move toward the same destination.

Thank you to the ADARA leadership, conference planning team, presenters, volunteers, exhibitors, sponsors, and attendees who contributed to such a meaningful experience. I would especially like to thank Dr. Kent Schafer, Ph.D. for his collective leadership and commitment to bringing people together across disciplines, perspectives, and lived experiences.

I leave ADARA 2026 with new ideas, renewed connections, and a deeper appreciation for the many ways we can continue moving our field forward—together.

Yesterday, I had the privilege of visiting the Georgia School for the Deaf and participating in the 2026 commencement.

This experience was especially meaningful to me because I never attended a Deaf school in the United States. I attended a Deaf school in Tokyo, Japan, from kindergarten through third grade, and then continued my education outside of Deaf schools through high school. Looking back, that journey came with social-emotional challenges and questions around identity, language, and belonging.

For this commencement, I intentionally chose not to use a transcript or PowerPoint. Instead, I wanted to share my reflections directly in ASL—not translated from English—and create space for authentic connection with the graduates and community.

It was inspiring to celebrate the graduates and spend time afterward connecting with students, staff, and administrators.

Moments like this remind me that representation, language, and community matter—and that there are many paths to belonging and growth.

Thank you to everyone who made this opportunity possible. I’m truly grateful.
