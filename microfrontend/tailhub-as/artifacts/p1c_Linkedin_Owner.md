Reposted from Ankur Bhadani:
I shut down a client's project on day one itself, not because the idea was bad but he said..."We just need an app. Nothing fancy."

That one line has killed more products than bad code ever will.

"Just an app" is how you end up with something that launches, gets ignored, and dies on the Play Store with eleven downloads and four of which are your own team running regression tests.

Here's what years of developing Mobile Apps apps actually taught me:
Nobody needs an app.
People need a problem solved.
The app is just the delivery mechanism.

The businesses that get this right don't start with a feature list or a tech stack. They start with one question;
What is the single thing our user needs to do and how do we make that feel effortless?

UX, architecture, API design, performance comes all of it from that answer.
Get that wrong at the beginning and no amount of good code fixes it later.

So make sure to, Define the problem first & Then Build it. Everything else is decoration.

The scariest thing I've seen in 18 years of building software wasn't a failed launch, but it was a successful product whose own team was scared to touch it.

Most people won't say this out loud but,
Half the "technical debt" problems I've walked into weren't caused by bad resources. They were caused by agencies who sold a vision, rushed the build, collected the payment and disappeared. Leaving behind a codebase that looked functional on the surface and was quietly collapsing underneath.

I've sat across founders who had no idea of:
- Paying customers
- Good reviews
- Growing numbers

And somewhere inside the system, a module nobody on the team would touch anymore. Not because they're not aware about how but, because the agency that built it never documented it, never handed it over properly, and was long gone before anyone noticed the cracks.

That's the part that genuinely bothers me. Because by the time a founder realizes what happened after they've already lost months.

Building software isn't just about writing code but, it's about building something your team can understand, modify and scale without that resource who coded it.

If your tech partner can't answer "What happens after you deliver?" That's your answer.

When companies think about modernization, they often imagine a complete rebuild.
- New systems.
- New architecture.
- Starting from scratch.

But that’s rarely the smartest move. Modernization isn’t about replacing everything. It’s about improving what exists — strategically.

In many cases, the most effective approach is incremental transformation.
Why? Because full rebuilds are:
- expensive
- time-consuming
- high-risk

And often unnecessary.

Instead, modern enterprises take a more practical approach:
1. Replace in phases: Modernize one component at a time without disrupting the entire system.
2. Build around the core: Keep stable systems intact and extend capabilities through integrations.
3. Gradual migration: Move workloads and features step by step instead of all at once.
4. Improve architecture over time: Refactor systems to become more modular and scalable.

This approach reduces risk while maintaining business continuity.

The goal of modernization isn’t to rebuild. It’s to create a system that can evolve continuously. Because systems that evolve don’t just survive change — they adapt to it.

Before planning a full rebuild, ask:
Can we evolve this system instead of replacing it?
