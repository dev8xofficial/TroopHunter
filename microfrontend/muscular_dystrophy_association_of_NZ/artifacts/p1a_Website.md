## 5 — Ministry for Disabled People

url: https://mda.org.nz/ministry-for-disabled-people/
type: article
meta: none

The Government announced transformative changes for disabled people, shared via a joint message from Minister for Disability Issues Carmel Sepuloni and Minister of Health Andrew Little.

### Key Announcements

- Establishing a Ministry for Disabled People to lead ongoing transformation of the disability system across government.
- Implementing the Enabling Good Lives (EGL) approach to Disability Services nationally.
- Introducing the Accessibility for New Zealanders Bill — new standalone legislation to progressively make Aotearoa more accessible.
- Establishing a new Accessibility Governance Board.

### Context

24% of New Zealanders identify as having a disability. Historically, disability support issues have been treated solely as health issues. The new Ministry will be a dedicated, autonomous agency to drive better outcomes, lead cross-government strategic disability policy, deliver and transform Disability Support Services, and progress system transformation work.

The EGL approach has been piloted in Christchurch, Waikato, and Mid Central regions. The Government acknowledged transformation will take time and requires a shift from deficit-focused to strengths-based models.

The new Accessibility framework will accelerate removal of systemic accessibility barriers, backed by legislation and the new Accessibility Governance Board. Disabled community voices will be embedded at all levels of decision-making. A dedicated Establishment Unit will manage the transition of some Ministry of Health functions.

## 14 — The benefits of gaming

url: https://mda.org.nz/the-benefits-of-gaming/
type: article
author: Scott Boyle
meta: none

### Background

Scott Boyle has SMA2, which causes progressive muscle weakness. In 2004 his paediatric neurologist recommended a games console to his parents as a way to preserve his hand function. From age 10, Scott played games nightly in a standing frame for one hour on school nights and two hours on weekends — a routine that lasted until age 16. The result: without daily controller use, his fingers would have no function.

### Physical and Cognitive Benefits

- VR gaming can support exercise and range of movement maintenance.
- A 2014 study by Davood G. Gozli in the journal Human Movement Science found that people who play action-centric games learn sensorimotor skills (such as riding a bike or driving) more quickly.

### Psychological and Social Benefits

Gaming communities have become positive outlets for people managing trauma and depression. Mats Steen, a Norwegian gamer with Duchenne muscular dystrophy who was thought to have lived in isolation, was mourned at his death by thousands of online gaming friends — many attended his funeral in person.

### Advice for Parents

Build a routine and stick to it. Potential negatives (addiction, microtransactions) are manageable with regulation. Multiple studies over 20 years have not supported claims linking gaming to violence.

Originally published in the Winter 2021 edition of In Touch magazine.

## 15 — Technology: Creating accessible solutions

url: https://mda.org.nz/technology-creating-accessible-solutions/
type: article
author: Genevieve McLachlan
meta: none

### What is Assistive Technology?

Assistive technology is any item, equipment, product, or software that enables a person with disabilities or physical limitations to live independently, maintain functions, or improve motor functions — from magnification software and speech dictation to specialist hardware. Access to technology is critical for building a fully inclusive society.

### Types of Assistive Technology

- Dragon Speech Recognition Software — converts voice to text; useful for people with dyslexia or those who cannot use their hands.
- ZoomText or SuperNova Magnification Software — magnifies the computer screen; for people with low vision.
- JAWS or Dolphin Screen Reading Software — reads the computer screen by voice; for blind users.

Apple, Microsoft, and Android increasingly incorporate such features into their operating systems, though significant access needs often require specialist assessment to determine the best solution. Government funding may be available for employed or studying individuals who need specialised assistive technology.

Genevieve McLachlan, founder of Adaptive Technology Solutions (www.adaptivetech.co.nz), uses assistive technology daily to run her business. Originally published in the Winter 2021 edition of In Touch magazine.

## 16 — Information pathways and Covid-19

url: https://mda.org.nz/information-pathways-and-covid-19/
type: article
author: Alan Royal
meta: none

Wellington SeniorNet facilitator Alan Royal writes about using mind maps to help older and impaired people navigate Covid-19 information during lockdowns.

Living in a retirement village — where 50% of residents over 65 have one or more impairments — Royal observed that lack of confidence with technology and difficulty locating information were major barriers during Covid-19. He developed a process using a mind map (a single-page diagram of web links) to present health and wellbeing information simply for older people.

The mind map is dynamic, updateable in real time, and received positive feedback: users reported gaining confidence to explore further. Lockdowns also provided opportunities to help residents learn to use search engines to find both technology help and health/exercise resources.

Royal notes the distinction between impairment and disability: an impairment only becomes a disability when technology, information, services, or the environment fail to resolve it.

Alan Royal is a member of Wellington City Council's Accessibility Advisory Group and a facilitator with Wellington SeniorNet. Originally published in the Winter 2021 edition of In Touch magazine.

## 17 — Disability support issues are not solely health issues

url: https://mda.org.nz/disability-support-issues-are-not-solely-health-issues/
type: article
author: Alison Riseborough
meta: none

Alison Riseborough, MDANZ representative on the DPO Coalition, provides an update on disability policy.

### Disability Support Services

The Health and Disability System Review had recommended transferring Disability Support Services (DSS) from the Ministry of Health to District Health Boards. Strong DPO opposition led the Government to reject this. Minister Andrew Little acknowledged that disability support issues are not solely health issues and span the full range of social issues. The Ministry of Health and Ministry of Social Development were commissioned to provide further advice on the future of DSS, based on Enabling Good Lives principles.

### DPO Coalition Meetings

DPO Coalition representatives met with Minister Carmel Sepuloni in March to discuss: clarity on access to Covid-19 vaccines; a clear implementation plan for Mental Health (He Ara Oranga); and direct engagement with the Health Reforms Transition Unit. The Government acknowledged earlier engagement with the disability community was poor.

### Health Reforms Transition Unit

In May the DPO Coalition began workshops with the Transition Unit, sharing disabled people's experiences of accessing health services.

### Accessibility Legislation

Regular engagement with the MSD policy team is ongoing to develop options for Accessibility Legislation. Advice on options was due to go to Cabinet later in 2021, with potential to set requirements across all of New Zealand society, business, and government for delivering accessibility on an equal basis.

Contact: alison.riseborough@mda.nz. Originally published in the Winter 2021 edition of In Touch magazine.

## 18 — Now, that's a great idea!

url: https://mda.org.nz/now-thats-a-great-idea/
type: article
meta: none

MDANZ members share favourite gadgets that improve their daily lives. Originally published in the Winter 2021 edition of In Touch magazine.

### Peggy Lake — Facioscapulohumeral MD — Ceiling Track Hoist (Maxi Sky 2)

After breaking her ankle and losing the ability to do a standing transfer, Peggy required full hoisting. An old-style manual hoist was difficult to manoeuvre even in a modified ensuite. After two years, her sister discovered a ceiling track hoist option. Peggy's occupational therapist recommended the Arjo Huntleigh Maxi Sky 2, funded through Enable with a $2,000 personal contribution for wall mounting (enabling full directional travel rather than left-right only). The Maxi Sky 2 allows a single carer to transfer her without manual lifting — lifted by button press, then glided with minimal push to bed, commode, or power chair. Carers stand to the side rather than in front, making the experience more dignified. Product: arjo.com.

Tip: Satin sheets, satin pyjamas, and a satin sling reduce friction and make transfers easier for both user and carer. A satin pillowslip under the hips assists rotation in bed; another on the power chair cushion enables position adjustments when seated.

### Heather Anderson — Charcot Marie Tooth Disease — Billy Shoes

Billy shoes have a zip that runs along the outside of the shoe and around the toe, allowing the upper to fold open completely so the foot can be placed unobstructed. Heather has wide feet with pulled-up arches, bent toes, and wears an ankle-foot orthosis — previously requiring oversized shoes. With fiddly fingers from muscle waste, laces were difficult. Billy shoes (kid's size 5 wide) fit properly and are easy to put on and take off. Available from Adaptive AF Footwear (small family business, Auckland fittings available).

### Mitchell Fels — Duchenne MD — Keeping Warm in Winter (reported by mother Jan Fels)

- Heated throw blanket — for use at a computer.
- Heated slippers — rechargeable batteries in an ankle pocket, three heat settings, last several hours on low. Described as a godsend for cold feet.
- Under The Weather Chairpod — fits over a wheelchair for outdoor cold months (utwpods.com).
- Hand warmer attached to wheelchair driving console — kept Mitchell driving in extreme cold, including New York in April (mo-vis.com).

### Denise Ganley — Limb Girdle MD — Powerfit Powerlegs

Powerfit Powerlegs is a vibration and acupressure system that improves circulation without exercise — important for wheelchair users who experience oedema (fluid accumulation in the legs and feet). Denise was hospitalised with Chronic Venous Insufficiency (CVI). Using Powerfit Powerlegs for approximately 20 minutes each evening has kept the oedema under control by promoting fluid drainage overnight.

### Lynda House — Cerebellar Ataxia — Decorated Walking Sticks

Lynda uses two walking sticks: a pink New Zealand–patterned stick with a hand cradle found via a Google search, and a metallic purple HurryCane with a claw base that can stand independently on level floors. The HurryCane was a gift from her daughter, decorated with earrings as cellphone charm replacements. Lynda coordinates stick colours with her hair, which her grandchildren colour frequently.

### Stages of Vaccine Development (CDC framework)

1. Exploratory stage
2. Pre-clinical stage
3. Clinical development (Phases I–IV)
4. Regulatory review and approval
5. Manufacturing
6. Quality Control

### Clinical Development Phases

- Phase I — Small groups of healthy volunteers; assesses tolerability and dosing.
- Phase II — Larger groups; evaluates safety and side effects in populations of interest.
- Phase III — Thousands of participants; double-blind randomised controlled trial comparing new vaccine against control (placebo or alternate). Demonstrates safety and efficacy.
- Phase IV — Post-approval ongoing evaluation to confirm real-world safety and effectiveness.

### Additional Considerations for NZ Covid-19 Vaccines

Medsafe evaluates all new medicines for approval. Licensure decisions consider: clinical and public health significance (including health equity); risk-benefit analysis; cost-effectiveness; and feasibility of equitable delivery.

type: article
meta: none

Written by the New Zealanders for Health Research team. Originally published in Autumn 2021 edition of In Touch magazine.

Key reasons to participate in clinical trials:

- You receive the best possible standards of care, even if assigned to a placebo group.
- If the trial is successful, you may gain early access to new medicines not yet available to others — and sponsoring organisations typically continue funding the treatment after the trial ends, important given Pharmac's funding delays.
- You improve your knowledge of your condition and contribute to future generations.
- You access up-to-date best practice clinicians who are thought to practice medicine approximately five years ahead of non-research colleagues.
- You free up clinical capacity, providing earlier access to treatment for others in your community.

## 30 — Telehealth wheelchair assessments under the microscope

url: https://mda.org.nz/telehealth-wheelchair-assessments-under-the-microscope/
type: article
author: Dr Fiona Graham
meta: none

Dr Fi Graham, Senior Lecturer in the Rehabilitation Teaching and Research Unit at the University of Otago, outlines research into Telehealth Wheelchair Assessment Service (T'WAS). Originally published in Autumn 2021 edition of In Touch magazine.

### Background

A colleague, occupational therapist Sally Wallace, was experimenting with telehealth (video-conferencing, photo-sharing, phone) for clients with complex wheelchair and seating needs several years before Covid-19. A scoping review of existing literature found minimal research on the topic, with lab testing suggesting equivalent results between in-person and telehealth wheelchair assessment. Clients were positive; assessors had concerns about data security and clinical accuracy.

### Research Study

With $17,000 from the Neuromuscular Research Fund (2018), the team studied the social and technical requirements for a T'WAS. Over 12 months, 117 people participated (47 wheelchair users, 49 assessors, 9 family members, 6 technicians, 3 service managers). Māori represented 12% of responses.

### Key Findings

- Most respondents thought T'WAS was a good idea but cautioned it should not be the only option.
- Assessors were enthusiastic despite low current usage.
- Wheelchair users (including Māori) were better equipped with technology than assessors.
- Tablets were optimal hardware; simple off-the-shelf software (e.g. Zoom) was preferred.
- The most-valued benefit was more timely assessment; frustration with the current in-person system was widely expressed.
- Māori responses were generally positive. One Māori assessor raised concerns about whanaungatanga via telehealth; Māori wheelchair users felt telehealth could improve cultural safety and create a more even playing field.

Next steps include further funding to map contexts in which T'WAS works or does not work, and create a freely accessible resource for assessors and wheelchair users. Publicly available workshops: youtube.com/watch?v=YebdoSmNAaU. Full articles: fi.graham@otago.ac.nz.

## 31 — The good and the bad of disability data

url: https://mda.org.nz/the-good-and-the-bad-of-disability-data/
type: article
meta: none

By Roger Loveless (advocate for disabled persons, MDANZ member) and Sam Murray (senior policy analyst, CCS Disability Action). Originally published in Autumn 2021 edition of In Touch magazine.

### The Problem with Disability Data

Quality disability data must acknowledge the human aspects of disability, including hidden costs. It is also a tool for holding government, society, and the private sector to account.

### Bad News

The 2018 Disability Survey was cancelled (originally held every five years since 1996). The next survey is planned for 2023, a 10-year gap from the 2013 survey — far too long given the survey's depth on impairment types, ethnicity, and gender.

### Good News

A growing number of regular surveys (Household Labour Force Survey, General Social Survey, Health Survey, 2018 Census, etc.) now include disability questions, though with fewer identification questions, smaller identified populations, and less breakout capability.

### Key Data Findings

- Household Labour Force Survey (June edition, disability data from 2017): no real change in employment outcomes for disabled people aged 15–64 since 2017. Employment rate remains under half that of non-disabled peers.
- 2018 data: disabled people under 65 were almost 2.5 times more likely to report insufficient income than non-disabled peers under 65; disabled people over 65 were 1.5 times more likely.
- Age complicates disability data: the disability rate rises sharply over 65, meaning disabled people have a higher median age than the general population, which can obscure inequalities experienced by those with lifelong impairments.

### Gaps

- No regular, reliable data on the financial and time costs disability imposes on individuals and whānau — including unpaid family support, private spending on equipment, and opportunity costs from reduced employment or volunteering.
- The 2023 Disability Survey may help, but regular data collection remains essential.

Surveys with current disability data: General Social Survey, Household Labour Force Survey, Te Kupenga, 2018 Census, NZ Crime & Victims Survey, Health Survey. Available at stats.govt.nz/topics/disability.

## 50 — COVID-19 and neuromuscular conditions

url: https://mda.org.nz/covid-19-and-neuromuscular-conditions/
type: article
date: 28 March 2020
meta: none

World Muscle Society (WMS) position and advice for people with neuromuscular disease (NMD), collated by Maxwell S. Damian, PhD. This document was subject to revision every 3 days from the date of issue.

### 1. Are people with NMD at higher risk?

National neurological associations define the risk of a severe Covid-19 course as high or moderately high in all but the mildest NMD forms. High/very high risk features include:

- Chest/diaphragm muscular weakness (FVC <60%), especially with kyphoscoliosis
- Ventilation via mask or tracheotomy
- Weak cough or oropharyngeal weakness
- Presence of tracheostoma
- Cardiac involvement or medication for cardiac involvement
- Risk of deterioration with fever, fasting, or infection
- Risk of rhabdomyolysis with fever, fasting, or infection
- Concomitant diabetes and obesity
- Steroid use or immunosuppressant treatment

### 2. Infection Prevention for High-Risk NMD Patients

- Social distancing of at least 2 metres minimum; self-isolation advised for high-risk individuals.
- Work from home or stagger working times if possible.
- Avoid large gatherings and public transport.
- Frequent hand-washing (20 seconds with soap and warm water); 60% alcohol-based hand sanitiser; surface disinfection.
- Caregivers should be in-house if possible. Essential visiting caregivers must wear appropriate PPE and face masks.
- Visiting physiotherapy discouraged; physiotherapists to provide remote advice.
- Plans must be in place for caregiver absence; hospitalisation should be avoided if possible.

### 3. Treatment Considerations

- Maintain at least a one-month supply of medication and ventilatory support equipment.
- DMD patients on steroids must not stop suddenly; steroid dose may need to increase during illness.
- Immunosuppression for inflammatory muscle disease, myasthenia gravis, and peripheral nerve disease should not be discontinued without specialist consultation.
- Hospital-based treatments (e.g. nusinersen/Spinraza, alglucosidase alfa/Myozyme, IVIg, rituximab) should not be stopped but moved to non-hospital settings where possible. IVIg can be changed to subcutaneous immunoglobulin. Clinical trials: consult trial centres.

### 4. Ventilatory Services During Isolation

- Patients should carry an alert card or medical bracelet with their Neuromuscular Centre contact.
- Neuromuscular Centres should actively contact patients on ventilatory support.

### 5. When to Seek Admission

Admission should be avoided if possible but not delayed when necessary. Key considerations:
- Emergency services may be under severe pressure.
- NMD may be incurable but not untreatable — this distinction must be made clear to emergency staff.
- Some hospitals prohibit use of patients' home NIV equipment.

### 6. Potential Interactions with Covid-19 Treatments

Chloroquine and azithromycin are unsafe in myasthenia gravis (unless ventilatory support is available). Some treatments may affect metabolic, mitochondrial, myotonic, and neuromuscular junction disorders. Experimental treatments should only be taken after consultation with a neuromuscular specialist.

### 7. Role of Neuromuscular Specialists

Must collaborate with Emergency and ICU teams; be available to ensure fair provision of intensive care; contribute to hospital policies and triaging algorithms; develop guidelines to keep patients at home as long as possible.

### 8. Patient Support From Neuromuscular Centres

- Patient hotlines staffed by specialist personnel with physician backup.
- Telemedical phone/video clinics (may require data security regulation modification).
- Outreach ventilatory support strategies.
- Liaison with ICU on home NIV equipment and mask systems with viral particle filters.

## 75 — September is Charcot-Marie-Tooth Awareness Month!

url: https://mda.org.nz/september-is-charcot-marie-tooth-awareness-month/
type: article
meta: none

CMT was named for Jean-Martin Charcot and Pierre Marie (Paris) and Howard Henry Tooth (Cambridge), who identified it in 1886. Also known as hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy (HMSN), CMT is a group of disorders affecting peripheral nerves — those outside the brain and spinal cord that supply muscles and sensory organs in the limbs. An estimated 700+ people in New Zealand live with CMT.

### Key Characteristics

CMT affects both motor and sensory nerves. Typical features include weakness of the foot and lower leg muscles (causing foot drop and a high-stepped gait), foot deformities (high arches, hammertoes), and later weakness and muscle atrophy in the hands affecting fine motor skills. Onset is most often in adolescence or early adulthood, though mid-adulthood onset also occurs. Severity varies greatly even within families. Progression is gradual. CMT is not considered fatal; most forms do not affect life expectancy. Foot/leg braces or orthopaedic devices may be needed to maintain mobility.
