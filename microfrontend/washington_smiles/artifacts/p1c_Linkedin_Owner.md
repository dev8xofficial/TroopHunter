Reposted from Dr. Brett Kessler

A few weeks ago I posted about speaking to a thousand dentists at a PDS Health conference. The reaction told me the post uncovered a passionate and emotional subject that needs more rational discussion. A few weeks have passed. I want to clarify my positions. Much of what was said about me in those threads was inaccurate.

I have been in leadership at all three levels of organized dentistry for over twenty years. I am proud of my tenure.

I am past president of the ADA. I am past president of the Colorado Dental Association. I have no leadership role in organized dentistry today. Because of my history in leadership, my views could be conflated as the organization's views.

When I share my views now, I speak for myself.

What I actually believe: The doctor patient relationship is our sacred cow. All treatment decisions should be in the spirit of the best possible options presented to our patients in the spirit of improving their health. All treatment decisions should be made between the doctor and the patient free of any outside influences. When I was president of the ADA, we built a clinical autonomy statement for exactly that reason. Practice owners, DSO structures, PE firms chasing returns: none of them get to dictate care.

On private equity: Private equity's goal is multiples for investors. Dentists' goals are to provide the best care for our patients. Those goals don't align. PE has the opportunity to be very dangerous in dentistry due to this value disconnect.

On DSOs: Great dentists do great work in DSOs, just like great dentists do great work in private practice. Bad actors exist in both. When I spoke at that conference, I was there for the dentists, to invite them under the ADA's tent, because they belong there. Many don't feel welcome. This must change.

On change: If we want change, we have to be in the room where it happens. That means talking to people or building relationships with organizations we don't always agree with in the spirit of establishing common ground.

On insurance reform: This has been a focus of mine for over ten years in leadership and most of my 30+ year practicing career. It will be the topic of another post.

Over the last two posts, I shared how we grew from one practice to multiple locations… and how we're building systems to better support our leaders.

Today, I want to get practical.

When we built our Integrity Dental Group Office Manager Playbook, we focused on 3 core areas that make or break a practice:

**1. Scheduling**

A full schedule means nothing if it's not the right schedule. We focus on:
- Productive scheduling, not just busy scheduling
- Balancing provider time, procedure mix, and patient flow
- Reducing gaps, last-minute cancellations, and inefficiencies

Because a well-built schedule doesn't just increase production, it reduces stress for the entire team.

**2. Managing the Day-to-Day**

This is where office managers live.
- Morning huddles that actually set the tone
- Real-time problem solving throughout the day
- Supporting the team, managing patient experience, and keeping everything moving

It's part leadership, part operations, part emotional intelligence. And no two days ever look the same.

**3. Collections**

This is where many practices struggle. We built clear expectations around:
- Daily collections and accountability
- Insurance follow-up and aging reports
- Financial conversations with patients that are clear and confident

Because a healthy practice isn't just about what you produce, it's about what you collect.

Beyond those three, we anchored everything around consistency: what needs to happen daily, what gets reviewed weekly, what gets measured monthly. Scorecards. KPIs. Team accountability. Clear communication.

The goal isn't perfection. It's clarity.

Because when your office manager knows exactly what matters and when it matters, everything runs smoother. Fewer fires. Less stress. Better results.

Simple. Clear. Repeatable.

Because when the systems are strong, your people can focus on what actually matters — taking care of patients.

If I tell you to close your eyes and imagine cutting into a fresh lemon. Seeing the juice bead up, smelling the citrus, and taking a bite, your mouth will likely start to water. There’s no lemon in the room, yet your body responds as if it’s real.

That’s the Lemon Test: your mind’s ability to create a physical response based purely on expectation.

Now apply that to your day in dentistry.

Before you ever pick up a handpiece, you’ve already written the story in your head. Maybe you’re expecting the patient who won’t get numb, the complainer, the patient who jumps during treatment, getting blamed for treatment failure that wasn’t your fault. And just like the lemon, your body and emotions respond to that expectation before anything even happens.

Try the opposite.

Walk in expecting people to be grateful. Expect that the work you’re doing, every exam, every restoration, every conversation is improving someone’s overall health. Because it is. Oral health impacts the entire body, and the smallest thing you do may make a bigger difference in someone’s life than you realize.

Shift the story you tell yourself, and you may find your entire day feels different.

Dentistry is challenging. But the narrative you choose to carry into it is powerful.

✨ Did sexism ever play a role? 👩‍⚕️

I get asked this question a lot. Did I encounter sexism or discrimination being a woman in a predominantly male industry as I was coming up?

First, I’m not naïve enough to think this doesn’t happen in our industry. I’ve seen examples of it, and I know others who have experienced it firsthand. I just never personally felt like I was truly targeted by it.

My honest answer to that question is that I’ve always believed people have different strengths and some of those are influenced by their sex. Using those strengths to my advantage has always been my goal.

In dentistry, I think women have many advantages.
We’re generally more compassionate providers, we lead our teams with empathy, and we’re often the ones patients specifically request. Those are powerful qualities in a field built on trust and care.

But reflecting on that question recently made me remember one of my early experiences when I moved to Washington, Missouri.

An older male dentist called me one day, furious about a patient.
The story doesn’t really matter, but I had placed a temporary filling in a deep cavity to evaluate if endo was needed.

A year later, the patient had disappeared, I guess it was successful since the patient was pain-free.

Later, that same patient told this dentist it was a permanent filling that had fallen out. He called me and told me my “filling should last longer than a year and I should give the patient her money back.”

Temporary fillings are meant to last a few weeks at best.

Now thinking back, I’m wondering:
1️⃣ Would he have called a male colleague new to town and spoken that way? Maybe
2️⃣ Was it because he was just an a$$hole? Perhaps
3️⃣ Or was it just his way with any new dentist in town, establishing his dominance? Likely

Not long after that, I shared the story with my neighbor — an established female dentist who had taught at Washington University Dental School before it closed.

I asked her if there was anything I should have done differently.
She smiled and said, “Oh, he’s just sexist. Don’t let it bother you.”

Side note: that same dentist and I eventually joined forces and her practice became the second practice I ever purchased. Funny how things come full circle.

That call, and that conversation, shaped how I would treat others entering this profession.

Anyone who knows me knows that I’ve made it a point to welcome new dentists to town with open arms…
To be generous with my time, my referrals, and my support because I remember how it felt to experience the opposite.

💬 A Pedicure Conversation That Speaks for Dentistry 💬

The young man giving me a pedicure last night asked me what I do. I sometimes don’t share and instead say I own a business— small town, public opinions, asking dental advice, and honestly, people rarely understand when a dentist says the job is hard.

Even though I was tired from a busy day, I told him: “I’m a dentist.”

We talked for a few minutes about procedures I specialize in and if it is hard. I told him about working upside down and backwards in the mirror. I said it was probably a lot like his job, it’s busy and you provide a service and then the day is over fast. It was easy and light conversation. Then he asked me what I like least about my job. I was kind of surprised by the deep question. And I think he was surprised by my answer.

He expected “bad breath” or “long hours.” And I considered using humor to deflect like I usually do in a casual conversation about dentistry. 
Maybe I was tired or maybe he seamed genuinely interested, but I told him the truth: the hardest part about my job is dental insurance.

➡️ Insurance companies still reimburse dentists at rates set in the 1980s.
➡️ Annual maximums haven’t increased since I graduated dental school 21 years ago.
➡️ And yet, patients are led to believe their “coverage” should work like medical insurance.

The result? Patients are confused, frustrated, and too often angry with the dentist or the office — when really, it’s the insurance system that is decades out of date.

The reality is this: dentistry hasn’t failed our patients. Dental insurance has.

As a profession, we need to keep saying this out loud. Not just to each other, but to our patients, our legislators, and anyone who will listen. Because until we stop accepting the status quo, nothing will change.

Our patients deserve better. Our doctors deserve better. And the profession we love deserves better.

I recently asked chat GPT a random question “why are dentists unhappy?”

Response:

Many dentists struggle with unhappiness for a variety of reasons that go beyond the surface perception of a well-paid, respected profession. Here are some of the most common reasons:

⸻

🧠 Mental Load & Isolation
 • High pressure, low support: Dentists are often expected to be perfectionists. One wrong move can have serious consequences. And yet, they’re rarely given adequate mentorship or emotional support.
 • Professional isolation: Especially in solo or high-volume offices, dentists can feel alone in their decision-making. They’re often the only clinician in the building responsible for major decisions.

⸻

💰 Financial Stress (Yes, Really)
 • Student debt: Many dentists graduate with $300,000+ in loans. That stress doesn’t go away, even with a high income.
 • Overhead costs: Running a practice is expensive. Lab fees, staff wages, equipment, and insurance add up—and can eat into the perceived income.
 • Pressure to produce: Associate dentists in DSOs or group practices are often under pressure to “produce” to meet quotas, and their income may be unpredictable or poorly explained.

⸻

🛠️ Loss of Autonomy
 • Corporate influence: Many dentists feel like they’re working in assembly lines under large DSOs, where patient care is secondary to profits.
 • Limited decision-making power: Associates often don’t get to choose the labs, materials, or schedules, which can feel demeaning for trained professionals.

⸻

⏳ Work-Life Imbalance
 • Long hours, after-hours charting, and managing emergencies or difficult patients can bleed into home life. Burnout is very common, especially without a strong team or leadership.

⸻

🧍‍♂️ Emotional Burden
 • Patient anxiety and distrust: Many patients arrive scared, skeptical, or already in pain. That tension wears on dentists over time.
 • Difficult conversations: Telling someone they need thousands of dollars of work they didn’t expect (or can’t afford) creates stress, guilt, or resentment—on both sides.

⸻

🧭 Lack of Purpose or Direction
 • Some dentists go through the motions for years without clarity on why they’re doing what they’re doing. Without mentorship or room to grow, the career can feel stagnant.

Stop calling dental insurance ‘insurance.’ 

Start explaining it for what it really is: a discount plan with a cap.

Patients expect dental insurance to work like medical insurance. 
Here’s why that’s a problem—and how you can fix it.

Most patients assume:

- They’ll meet a deductible, and then insurance will cover the rest.
- That’s how medical insurance works, after all.

But dental insurance is completely different:

- It has an annual cap (usually $1,500–$2,000).
- Beyond that, patients pay out of pocket for everything else.

By explaining this up front, you:

1. Set realistic expectations.
2. Build trust with your patients.
3. Help them see the value in investing in their oral health, beyond insurance.

Dental insurance isn’t traditional insurance—it’s more like a discount membership card.

No one likes a difficult conversation...

These 8 reframes make them easier.

The biggest myth in difficult conversations---that the right words will magically appear in the moment.

They won't. 

But these practical swaps will help you navigate them with confidence:

1️⃣ Instead of: "We need to talk..." 
Say: "I'd like to share some observations and get your perspective." 
Why: Reduces immediate defensive reactions and shows you value two-way dialogue.

2️⃣ Instead of: "The problem is..." 
Say: "Here's the situation as I understand it..." 
Why: Creates space for different viewpoints without assigning blame.

3️⃣ Instead of: "You always/never..." 
Say: "I've noticed that recently..." 
Why: Focuses on specific instances rather than character judgments.

4️⃣ Instead of: Starting with complaints 
Say: "My goal for this conversation is..." 
Why: Sets a constructive tone and clear direction.

5️⃣ Instead of: "You made me feel..." 
Say: "When [situation happens], I feel..." 
Why: Takes ownership of your emotions while clearly linking them to specific actions.

6️⃣ Instead of: Avoiding silences 
Say: "Let's take a moment to consider this." or “Lets come back to this” 
Why: Gives both parties time to process and respond thoughtfully.

7️⃣ Instead of: Pushing for immediate solutions 
Say: "What options do you see for moving forward?" 
Why: Invites collaboration rather than forcing outcomes.

8️⃣ Instead of: Ending vaguely 
Say: "Let's agree on next steps and check in [specific time]." 
Why: Creates accountability and clear path forward.

These phrases are particularly relevant where there is a power dynamic at play.
Remember---difficult conversations become easier when you focus on clarity over comfort.
