## B.1a — THE CONSULTATION FEE (how to set it, how to say it)

This section governs every mention of money in this document. It exists because the
fee is the single point where a warm, trusting prospect most often evaporates — and
because almost every instinct a nervous seller has about pricing is wrong.

### The question this answers first: should we just let them pay what they think it's worth?

**No — and understanding why is the key to the whole section.**

The appeal is obvious: it removes all risk for the client, so surely nobody walks
away. But under this pipeline's post-paid model, **you have already given them that.**
The guarantee says: do the session, and if it wasn't worth the fee, you pay nothing
and keep the deliverable. The client is *already* the sole judge of value. There is
no additional safety left to buy by removing the number — there is only the anchor
left to lose. Adding "pay whatever you think" on top is paying twice for the same
objection and receiving less than nothing.

What removing the number actually costs you:

- **It replaces an easy decision with a hard one.** With a price, the client answers
  *yes or no.* Without one, they must **appraise a stranger's professional work** and
  name a figure — which risks looking cheap, or foolish, in front of someone whose
  opinion they now respect. That is a genuinely uncomfortable task, and the reliable
  human response to an uncomfortable task is deferral. *Never hand the client a hard
  job when an easy job produces the same outcome.*
- **It removes the only quality signal available.** Dev8X has no portfolio the client
  can inspect, no references they know, no logo they recognise. Under that much
  uncertainty, **price is the primary quality signal**, and an absent price is read as
  an absent conviction: *he doesn't know what this is worth either.*
- **It destroys the ladder.** The consultation fee is the anchor for the demo, and
  the demo is the anchor for the build. Tell a client once that your prices are
  whatever they feel like, and every subsequent number is a negotiation that starts
  from zero. Nobody pays five figures for a build to someone who let them name the
  price of the diagnosis.
- **It changes the category.** A named fee makes this a professional service. An
  unnamed one makes it a tip. Pay-what-you-want works in anonymous, low-stakes,
  high-volume settings — a café, a download. This is a high-trust B2B relationship
  where the buyer is deciding whether you are a peer.

**The correct structure is what you already have:** a firm, stated number, with the
client as final judge of whether it gets paid. That *is* "pay what you think is
appropriate" — with the dignity, the anchor, and the quality signal intact.

### The reframe that makes the number safe to say

Under post-paid, the fee is not a price. **It is a ceiling.**

Say it as one: *"The most it can cost you is X — and only if you tell me it was worth
more than that."* This is not spin; it is the literal, accurate description of the
deal on offer. It converts the scariest sentence in the message into the most
reassuring one, and it is the reason the number can be stated plainly rather than
apologised for. Every fee mention in this document must carry the ceiling frame.

Because the ceiling frame does the risk-reversal work, **the price and the guarantee
must live in the same sentence.** Separated, the price reads as real and the guarantee
reads as a disclaimer bolted on afterwards. Fused, the price *is* conditional from the
moment it is heard.

### How to SET the fee — the method

Do not pick a number. Derive it, in this order.

**Step 1 — Choose the reference class. This is the most consequential decision in
Dev8X's pricing, and it is made once per client.**

Every price is understood by comparison to a category the buyer already knows. The
buyer chooses that category automatically and instantly. Your only leverage is to
choose it *for* them, before they do.

- **Never let the reference class be developer time.** "Two hours of a developer" is a
  commodity comparison, it is globally shoppable, and it caps the build price by
  implication. A studio priced in dev-hours will be bought in dev-hours forever.
- **The reference class is advisory work the client already pays for and respects.**
  Read the research for what they actually buy: a business coach, an implementation
  consultant, a CE course, an operating-framework rollout, a fractional executive.
  These are non-commodity, four-figure, respected categories, and the buyer has
  already made peace with paying for them.

> Washington Smiles: Dr. Smith pays a **business coach** (Post 44 — she names them),
> pays for **CE and mentorship programs**, and is running an **operating framework**
> (Post 119 — `#rockefellerhabits`, `#DEO`). That is the reference class. A diagnostic
> session that produces a written operational diagnosis sits naturally beside an EOS
> or Rockefeller implementer's engagement — *not* beside a freelance web developer.
> Price it there, or you have surrendered the category.

**Step 2 — Set the package price at or just below one unit of that reference class.**
Parity signals peer. Slightly below signals accessible. Far below signals inferior
and invites the buyer to wonder what is wrong. Never far below.

**Step 3 — Let complexity move the package, not the rate.** Per Decision 9 the
consultation is one fixed package at one price. Complexity therefore determines **how
many sessions are in the package**, and the price moves in **discrete bands.** A
continuously-computed price invites negotiation, because a number that was calculated
can be re-calculated. A banded price reads as a rate card, and rate cards are accepted.

| Band | When it applies | Package |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | One operation, one decision-maker, a single system in question | 1 session, 1 named deliverable |
| **B** | Multi-location or multi-system, still a single decision-maker | 2 sessions, 2 named deliverables |
| **C** | Multiple stakeholders who must be aligned (partners, board, a CTO) | 3–4 sessions, deliverable per session |

> Washington Smiles is **Band B**: five locations and several systems, but Dr. Smith
> is the sole strategic decision-maker (Decision Card §4, §9).

**Step 4 — Check the floor, then check the ceiling.**

- **Floor.** `MEMORY.md §6` puts token cost per *acquired* consultation at roughly
  $200–560, before Abdul's ~2.5 hours. A fee at $500 is therefore not a price — it is
  **break-even**, and it means the entire front end of the business runs at a loss on
  Abdul's labour. The floor is the number at which one paid consultation comfortably
  funds the several unpaid pipelines that produced it. Treat anything at or near
  break-even as a mispriced offer, not a safe one.
- **Ceiling — the Discretionary Threshold.** The fee must sit **below the number at
  which the buyer needs anyone else's approval.** Cross that line and you have not
  raised your price; you have added a stakeholder, a meeting, and three weeks
  (see Branch P). For an owner-operator with budget authority this threshold is high,
  but the *psychological* threshold — "a thing I decide without thinking about it" —
  is much lower than the legal one, and it is the one that matters.

**Step 5 — Choose the digits.** Round thousands ($2,000) read as arbitrary, and
arbitrary numbers invite counter-offers. A number precise to the hundred ($1,800)
reads as computed, and computed numbers get accepted or declined rather than
negotiated. Do not go finer than that — a service priced at $1,847 reads as a stunt.

**Step 6 — Write the number and the reasoning into the tracking row, before Message 2
is sent.** A fee invented in the moment of writing the message will be a fee that
flinches.

### How to PRESENT the fee — the rules

1. **Ceiling frame, always.** "The most it can cost you is X." Never "the fee is X."
2. **Price and guarantee in one sentence.** Never adjacent, never separated.
3. **Say the number exactly once.** A repeated price grows in the reader's mind.
4. **Never the last line.** End on the next step, not the money. The final sentence
   is what the message is remembered as.
5. **Never justify it.** No "which reflects the depth of research involved." An
   unjustified price sounds settled; a justified one sounds negotiable. State it,
   then move on in the same breath.
6. **Never say "fee," "charge," "cost," or "investment."** The first three are the
   language of expense; the fourth is the language of salesmen. Say what it is:
   *"the most it can cost you is X."*
7. **Never anchor against competitors.** Anchor against the stakes number, or — when
   no figure exists — structurally (see *Anchoring Without a Number*). A bare price,
   anchored to nothing, is the one thing you may never send.
8. **Name the deliverable as an object.** Not "a session" but *"a written Operational
   Diagnosis."* A thing you can hold justifies a price; an hour of someone's time
   does not.
9. **Price the package, never the session.** "The whole thing is X." A per-session
   figure gets silently multiplied by an unknown session count.
10. **Never discount. If you must move, move scope.** Drop a session and drop the band;
    hold the rate. A discounted price is a confession that the original was invented,
    and it re-prices every future gate downward — including the build.
11. **Use the frozen time unit** from the Alignment Rule for any stakes figure quoted
    beside the price.

### MESSAGE 3 — Soft follow-up if no reply after Message 2
**Timing:** 3–4 days after Message 2.
- Lightly re-surface the core stakes (same number/phrasing as the video and
  Message 2). Optionally add ONE small new public observation IF a genuine one
  exists — do NOT imply insight was withheld earlier (that contradicts the
  video's Beat 3 honesty).
- Restate the guarantee in half a sentence.
- Make saying "not now" easy.

Length: 2–3 sentences.

> "No pressure at all — just circling back once. [Bottleneck] doesn't get smaller on
> its own, and nothing's owed unless you finish the session and tell me it was worth
> it. If the timing's off, say so and I'll leave it there."

**Do not restate the number.** He has it. Repeating a price in a follow-up is the
tell of someone who thinks the price was the problem, and it makes the figure grow
(§B.1a rule 3). Restate the *ceiling condition* instead — "nothing's owed unless" —
which is the part that actually removes his risk.

**End on the exit, not the ask.** "Say so and I'll leave it there" is p3b Rule 5, and
a follow-up that grants permission to decline is far more likely to receive a reply
than one that asks again.

**Maximum ONE Message 3.** If no reply, STOP. Re-engage in 90 days with a
fresh industry insight (not about them).

## B.2 — The War-Game Matrix (every scenario, exact response)

Not every prospect follows the happy path. This section fulfils the
Objection-Playbook coverage the pipeline needs — every branch below is a
ready-to-send response, personalized with this client's specifics and, where a
branch references "the video," quoting exactly what the finalized video said.

### BRANCH A — Correction: "You got it wrong"

Beat 5 explicitly invited this, and p3b Rule 5 gave an explicit permission to dismiss.
**A correction is therefore not an objection — it is the prospect accepting your
invitation.** They did the work of replying to a stranger to set the record straight
about their own business. That is the behaviour of someone who has decided you might
be worth their time. Mishandle it and you lose a prospect who was, at that moment,
closer to buying than a polite "interesting, thanks."

**The single most important rule in this branch: do not bridge to the offer in the
same message as the correction.**

The instinct — *"that's exactly why the session matters"* — converts their correction
into your sales argument in the same breath they gave it. A sharp CEO reads that
instantly: *I told him he was wrong and he used it to sell me.* Every point of trust
the correction earned is spent in one sentence, and it confirms the suspicion the
video worked so hard to disarm.

**Message 1 of 2 — absorb, and nothing else.**
Do not defend. Do not qualify. Do not explain your reasoning. Do not thank them so
profusely that it reads as a technique. Say what you got wrong, in their words, and
ask one genuine question about the thing you got wrong.

> "Then I had that backwards — thank you. [Restate the correction in his words, so he
> can see it landed.] What is the thing that actually costs you, then?"

**Then stop and let him answer.** The bridge, if it comes, belongs in the *next*
message, and only after he has told you more. Earning the right to make the offer is
what this pause buys.

**Message 2 of 2 — only after he responds, and only if he engaged.**
> "That's a different problem than the one I flagged, and a more expensive one. From
> outside I'll always have blind spots — [name the specific one he just exposed]. The
> session is where my guesses get replaced with your numbers."

**Handling the number.** If the correction invalidates the stakes figure, **retire it
out loud** — "then that number is wrong, and I'm dropping it" — and do not quote it
again in any later message or branch. Never silently substitute a new figure to
replace it: an invisibly-revised number is worse than a retracted one, and the
Alignment Rule forbids re-deriving one here. If a *replacement* figure is needed, it
comes from him (Branch U), not from you.

**Capture what he gave you.** A correction is an L1 upgrade — record it verbatim, with
attribution, for `p3d`. It is more valuable than anything the research produced,
because it is confirmed.

**If the correction guts the entire diagnosis** ("we don't even do that"): do NOT push
the offer, and do not attempt a second message. Acknowledge plainly, ask one genuine
question if you have one, and leave. A forced offer on a diagnosis he just disproved
destroys the one asset this pipeline is built to accumulate. Exit as Branch D.

**Do not confuse this with Branch O.** Branch A is *"that fact is wrong."* Branch O is
*"those facts are right, but the real problem is something else."* The second is a
better outcome and gets a different response.

### BRANCH B — Silent Watcher: watched, no reply (the modal outcome)
Most prospects watch, are impressed, and never reply — replying costs effort,
silence costs nothing.
**ONE touch only, 4–6 days after the video:**
> "Is [bottleneck] actually what's slowing [their goal]? No need to reply to
> the video itself — just curious if I'm off."
- **Same 7-word-preview rule as the wrapper (`p3b` Part 0.1):** this lands in
  a dormant thread, so lead with the specific tease, not the reply-bar
  mechanics. "No need to reply to the video" as an opener wastes the visible
  preview on meta-commentary instead of the hook — put the actual question
  first, put the low-friction framing second.
- Lower the reply bar to near zero — a yes/no answer.
- Do NOT re-pitch or repeat the video. Lead with ONE new observation or a
  single sharp question.
- **Maximum ONE** silent-watcher touch. Further messages read as pressure.

📌 **When you cannot tell whether they watched — assume they did.** LinkedIn does not
reliably report whether a DM video was played, so the B/C distinction often rests on a
signal Abdul does not actually have. Default to Branch B. Its message works either
way, whereas Branch C's "not sure if the video came through" told to someone who
*did* watch and chose not to reply is mildly insulting, and reveals you are guessing.

### BRANCH C — Didn't Watch: video genuinely not opened

**Use only when the non-watch is confirmed** (an unopened attachment, a bounced
thread). Otherwise use Branch B.

> "Not sure if the video came through — it's a 3-minute look at [one specific
> problem I found in your operation]. Thought it was worth flagging. Either
> way, no pressure."

📌 **This branch is documentation, not an extra send.** The one permitted not-watched
touch is `p3b`'s second wrapper (see *Ownership of the not-watched path* in B.3). If
that wrapper has gone out, this branch is already spent. Stop, and go to 90-day
re-engagement.

### BRANCH D — "Not Interested"
> "Completely fair. If anything changes, I'm easy to find. Wishing you well with
> [Company]."
Graceful, immediate exit. No persuasion, no "are you sure?" Door stays open.
Re-engage in 90 days ONLY with a general industry insight (not about them).

📌 Do **not** say "the research I did is still valid" or otherwise remind him what you
spent on him. At the exact moment he has declined, that sentence lands as a small bill
(p3b Rule 11) and converts a clean exit into a faint resentment. The shorter this
message is, the more likely he comes back.

### BRANCH E — "Too Busy Right Now"

Almost always true, and almost always also a soft no. Treat it as true — but do not
hand a man who just said he has no time a **scheduling task.** "When would be a better
time?" is homework. He will not do it, and the thread dies looking like his fault.

**Propose the date yourself and make the reply one word.**
> "Makes sense — this isn't urgent. I'll check back in [specific month]. If that's
> wrong, tell me when."

Then actually check back, exactly then, referencing this message. A follow-up that
arrives precisely when it said it would is itself a proof of competence, and it is
one of the few you can make without a portfolio. If he names a time, schedule it and
follow up on that date instead.

### BRANCH F — "What's This Going to Cost Me?"
A buying signal disguised as an objection.

**Answer the question. Do not bridge, do not defer, do not "explain more if you're
curious."** A direct price question deflected is the single most recognisable
salesman move there is, and it costs more trust than any number could. Someone who
has watched a video about their own operational blind spots and then asks what it
costs is not fishing — they are deciding, and they have just told you so.

Under the ceiling frame this is easy, because the honest answer is reassuring:

> "Nothing for what you've already had. If we do the working session, the most it can
> cost you is [CONSULTATION_FEE] — and only if, at the end, you tell me it was worth
> more than that. Nothing up front, and you keep the written [named deliverable]
> either way."

Then let it sit. Do not stack the stakes anchor, the legitimacy line and the ask on
top of a question that asked for one fact — that turns an answer into a pitch. If he
engages, Message 2 follows naturally and can carry the rest.

📌 If the fee has not yet been set for this client (§B.1a), **you are not ready to
send the video.** This question always arrives eventually.

📌 Never say the research was "free" or otherwise price your own effort — see p3b
Rule 11. "Nothing for what you've already had" states the fact without invoicing him
for it.

### BRANCH G — "Send Me More Info"

**Read this one carefully before responding — it has two entirely different meanings.**

*Genuine interest* sounds specific: they name a thing they want to see. *Polite
deflection* sounds vague — "send me some info" is the most common way a busy person
ends a conversation without saying no. Answering a deflection with an attachment
buries the thread in an unopened PDF and you never hear from him again.

**If vague, do not send anything. Ask what he actually wants to know.**
> "Happy to — what would be most useful? The detail behind one of those problems, or
> how the session itself works?"

A question is harder to ignore than an attachment, and his answer tells you which
objection is really live.

**If specific, send the Decision Safety Brief (p3a)** — he watched it on screen but
has never held it — plus one paragraph, and nothing else.
> "Here's the brief itself, so you can read it properly rather than watch me scroll
> it. [One sentence naming the single thing you'd most want him to look at.]"

📌 Do not describe it as "a one-page summary." It is a dense research dashboard, and
promising a summary then delivering a dashboard is a small broken promise at the
moment credibility is being assessed. Describe what it actually is.

**Follow up once, in 3–4 days:** "Did anything in there surprise you, or did it match
what you're already seeing?"

### BRANCH H — "I Already Have a Developer / Agency"

**Do not surrender the offer here — the premise is wrong.** Having a developer is not
a reason to skip a diagnosis, any more than owning a car is a reason to skip a
mechanic's inspection. **The consultation is advisory work, not development work.** It
produces a written diagnosis of an operation; it does not compete with whoever writes
the code. Conceding the whole engagement because they have a builder confuses your own
reference class (§B.1a Step 1) — and if *you* believe the consultation is a
development service, so will they, and it will be priced like one forever.

> "Good — you'll need one. What I do before that is different: the session is about
> working out what's actually worth building and what it's costing you not to have
> it. Plenty of people take that diagnosis to the developer they already have. If
> yours is the right person to build it, that's a good outcome, not a lost one."

That last sentence is the load-bearing one. It is true, it costs you nothing at this
stage, and it removes the only reason he had to end the conversation.

**If he still declines:** exit as Branch D. You've planted a seed that often converts
3–6 months later when the current provider disappoints — and it converts to you,
because you were the one who didn't try to displace them.

📌 Do not offer to send the brief to his agency. It is your research, it names his
bottleneck, and handing it to a competing vendor is a gift to them, not to him.

### BRANCH I — "Too Expensive" (after Message 2)
**Almost never about the money.** "Too expensive" from a founder who watched a
three-minute diagnosis of his own operation usually means *"I don't yet believe it
will be worth it."* Answering the literal sentence with a price defence answers a
question he did not ask. Answer the belief, and let the ceiling do the rest.

> "Fair. Though the part I'd push back on gently: you don't decide whether to pay
> until after it's done, and you decide it, not me. If it isn't worth more than that
> to you, you say so and you keep the written [named deliverable] anyway. The
> [bottleneck] costs you [stakes number, frozen unit] for as long as it stays — that's
> the number I'd want us to replace with a real one."

📌 **Do not restate the figure he just called expensive** (§B.1a rule 3). He knows it.
Naming it again is arguing about the price, which is the one argument you cannot win
and do not need to have.

📌 **Do not discount, and do not offer a smaller package to rescue the moment.** If
scope must move, it moves in a later, calmer message — never as a reflex to the word
"expensive," which teaches him that pushing moves your prices.

📌 Use the **frozen time unit** from the Alignment Rule. If the video said "per
year," this branch says per year. If the video carried no number, drop the figure and
run the structural anchor — never let a price objection tempt you into inventing one.
If still no:
> "Completely understand. The offer and the research stay valid. If the
> numbers change or the bottleneck gets worse, I'm here."
Never discount — discounting signals the original price was inflated. Anchor
against the stakes number, not competitors.

### BRANCH J — "I Want a Free Call Instead"

**This branch has two turns, and collapsing them destroys the paid gate.** The
current instinct is to hold the line *and* hand over the free call in the same
message — which means every prospect who says the word "free" gets one, and the
consultation stops being a gate at all. Under post-paid, the request is also usually
answered by a fact rather than a concession: **there is nothing to lose by booking
the session, because nothing is collected until he says it was worth it.**

**Turn 1 — answer the fear, offer nothing.** Do not mention the fit check.
> "I get it — paying a stranger feels like a risk. Here's the thing though: you
> don't pay anything up front. We do the session, and at the end you tell me
> whether it was worth it. If it wasn't, you keep the written diagnosis and pay
> nothing. There's no deposit and nothing to get back."

Most of this objection dissolves here, because most people asking for a free call
are protecting against *prepaying a stranger*, not against the session itself.

**Turn 2 — only if he pushes a second time**, and only if he is clearly interested
but stuck on trust rather than on value:
> "If it'd help to hear my voice before you commit to anything, I'll do 15 minutes
> — a fit check, not a diagnosis. If it's not a fit, I'll say so."

If he is stuck on **value** rather than trust ("I'm not sure this is worth
anything"), a free call will not fix that and will burn an Abdul-hour. Route to
Branch D and exit gracefully. (The fit-check script lives in
`p3d_Consultation_Playbook.md` §F.8, generated once the call is actually scheduled.)

**Minimum-viable fit-check structure (use this if the call happens before
`p3d_Consultation_Playbook.md` exists — this is a bridge, not a replacement for
§F.8; discard it once §F.8 is generated):**
1. Open with the same spirit as the video's reply ask: ask which flagged
   problem matters most to him, and why now — this keeps the call anchored to
   what he already told you in Message 1, not a fresh pitch.
2. Answer ORIENTING questions only — how the paid session works, what the
   guarantee covers, what he keeps either way. Never answer DIAGNOSTIC
   questions (his numbers, a specific recommendation, "what would you build")
   on this call — redirect those with the Branch S line below.
3. Close with a direct yes/no, not a soft maybe: "Does this feel worth the
   paid session, or should I leave it here?"
If the call drifts into free diagnosis at any point, treat it as Branch S
mid-call and stop answering substantive questions.

### BRANCH K — "Let Me Think About It"
> "Of course — it's a real decision and I respect that. One thing to sit with:
> the [bottleneck] costs [stakes number, frozen time unit] for as long as it stays
> as-is. The session is designed to pin that number down exactly. Either way, no
> rush."
**Follow up in 5–7 days:** "Hey — just checking in. Any questions I can
answer? Happy to help you think it through."
If still no reply: stop. 90-day re-engagement with fresh industry insight.

### BRANCH L — "Can You Send References?"

> "Absolutely. [Provide one real reference — a result, a testimonial, a
> previous project outcome]. If you'd like to speak with [previous client]
> directly, I can connect you."

**If there are no references yet, say so.** Do not deflect, do not reach for "we're a
focused studio," and above all do not manufacture a client. This is the same rule that
removed the proof-of-competence line from the video (`p3b`): an unverifiable claim
next to a price is the most expensive sentence you can write, and the entire reason a
stranger is still reading is that he has not yet caught you overstating anything.

> "Not the kind you'd want — I'd rather tell you that than dress something up. What I
> can point at is the work you've already seen: I spent real time on your operation
> before asking you for anything. And you don't pay unless you tell me the session was
> worth it, so you're not taking my word for any of this."

A candid "no" here is worth more than a weak "yes," because it is the first
independent confirmation that Beat 3's honesty was not a technique.

### BRANCH M — "Can You Show Me Examples of Your Work?"

Different from references — they want to SEE deliverables.

> "Happy to. Here's an example of [a relevant project — what was built, the
> problem it solved, one measurable result]. The Decision Safety Brief I built
> for you is itself a sample of how we work — we research first, then build."

**If there is no portfolio, do not say "we're selective about what we take on."** It
is a dodge, it sounds exactly like a dodge, and a founder who has hired people before
has heard it. Name the situation and redirect to the one artifact that is real:

> "Honestly, not much I can show you yet that would mean anything for your situation.
> The brief is the real sample — that's how we work before we build anything. If
> that's not enough to go on, I'd completely understand."

That last clause is the permission to dismiss (`p3b` Rule 5), and it belongs here more
than anywhere else in this document.

### BRANCH N — "Who Are You?" / "How Did You Find Me?"

> "I run Dev8X — a small development studio that builds web platforms and
> automation systems for businesses. I'd been reading your posts for a few weeks and
> commenting on some of them before I ever sent the connection request; [specific
> post] is what made me actually go look at how [Company] runs. The video is the
> result of that."

Always be honest about the discovery path. Never say "our algorithm found you" or
anything that sounds automated — this is a trust-check, and it is the only question in
this document where a *vague* answer is worse than an unflattering one.

📌 **Name the warming, don't hide it.** By this point he has seen Abdul's name on his
own posts several times (`p3b` Part 0.4) and accepted a connection request. An answer
that omits that is technically true and reads as evasive the moment he scrolls back
and finds the comments. Referencing them costs nothing and closes the loop honestly:
he was never approached cold, and now the record says so.

📌 Never answer this with "I don't do mass outreach" as an opener. Defending against an
accusation he has not made plants it.

### BRANCH O — "Actually, Our Real Problem Is [Something Different]"
Valuable — it means they're thinking about their business with you.
> "That's really useful to know — from outside, [the problem you identified]
> looked like the main bottleneck, but if [their stated problem] is what's
> actually costing you, that changes the picture. Can you tell me more about
> how it's affecting things day-to-day?"
Never defend the original diagnosis. Absorb the new problem, ask one follow-up
question. If solvable with web/automation: bridge to the consultation with the
updated framing. If NOT (hiring, management, compliance):
> "That sounds more like a [hiring / operations / management] challenge than a
> systems one. I don't want to force a tech solution where it doesn't fit. If
> there's a web or automation angle to it, I'd love to explore that — but if
> not, I'd rather be straight about it."

### BRANCH P — "I Need to Check With My Partner / Board / CTO"

📌 **Read this branch as pricing feedback first.** If the Decision Card says the
prospect is the sole decision-maker (as it does for Washington Smiles, §4 and §9) and
they nonetheless need to check with someone, the fee has crossed their **discretionary
threshold** (§B.1a Step 4). You did not raise your price; you added a stakeholder, a
meeting, and three weeks. Record this — it recalibrates the band for every future
client of this size, and it is the most valuable pricing signal the pipeline can
produce. Do **not** rescue it by discounting now (§B.1a rule 10); the information is
worth more than this one deal.

> "Of course — that makes sense. Would it help if I put together a short
> summary they can review? One page covering what I found and what the session
> would cover. That way they have context before you discuss it."
If yes: send a one-page summary (headline, top 3 problems, stakes number, what
the consultation delivers) — keep it forwardable. If the client has already paid
for and experienced a session, the **Engagement Journey one-pager**
(`p3e_Engagement_Journey.md`) is the purpose-built forwardable artifact for this
— it is self-contained and shows the whole path with only the consultation
package priced. Do not forward it to a still-cold prospect (see the timing rule
in `p3e`).
> Post-payment template line: "Here's a short page that walks through exactly
> how we work together, step by step. Happy to jump on a call with both of you
> if anything's unclear."
If "I'll just tell them":
> "Got it. If any questions come up, happy to jump on a quick call with both of
> you — sometimes it's easier to hear it directly."
**Follow up in 5–7 days:** "Did you get a chance to discuss with
[partner/team]? Any questions I can help with?"
Never pressure — multi-stakeholder decisions take time. If the partner says
no: graceful exit, door open, 90-day re-engagement.

### BRANCH Q — "We're Already In a Contract With Someone Until [Date]"
> "That's totally fair — no point in doubling up. When does the contract wrap
> up? I'm happy to reconnect closer to that date. The research I've done stays
> valid, and we can pick up right where we left off."
Note the contract end date; set a reminder for 30 days before. Re-engage with
a fresh insight at that time (not just "your contract is ending"). One of the
highest-conversion re-engagement segments — they were interested, just locked
in.

### BRANCH R — Prospect Forwards to Someone Else
A POSITIVE signal — they're bringing others into the conversation.
> "Absolutely — please do. If it's helpful, I can send them a short summary
> that gives context without needing the full video. And if they have
> questions, I'm happy to jump on a quick call with both of you."
Always encourage forwarding. Offer to engage the new person directly — don't
make the original prospect your salesperson. If the forwarded person reaches
out: treat them as a warm lead.
If this happens AFTER a paid session, swap the summary for the Engagement
Journey one-pager (`p3e_Engagement_Journey.md`) — it's built exactly for this:
> "Here's a short page that walks through exactly how we work together, step
> by step. Happy to jump on a call with both of you if anything's unclear."
Pre-payment, keep sending the light summary — `p3e` is never forwarded to a
still-cold contact (see the timing rule in `p3e`).

### BRANCH S — Free Consulting Extraction
The prospect keeps asking detailed questions to get free advice without
committing ("So what would you recommend?", "What tool should we use for X?").
**Detection signals:** multiple detailed questions after Message 1 with no
movement toward Message 2; questions asking for the consultation's
deliverables; a "just one more question" pattern.

📌 **Do not fire this branch on the first question.** A prospect asking "so what would
you build?" once is *buying* — he is imagining the thing, which is what you want. Two
or three such questions with no movement is extraction. Deploying Branch S against
genuine curiosity punishes the exact behaviour that precedes a yes, and it feels, to
him, like being told he is trying to cheat you. **One substantive question gets one
generous, non-specific answer.** The branch begins at the second.
> "Great question — that's exactly the kind of thing the session is built to
> answer properly. I could give you a quick take here, but the honest answer
> is it depends on your numbers, and I'd rather give you the right answer than
> a fast one. That's what the session is for."
Never be rude or accusatory — most people don't realize they're doing it. The
line: factual observations about their problem = free; specific
recommendations, solutions, or architecture = paid. If it continues: "I want
to give you a proper answer, not a corridor one. The session is where I can do
that." Then stop answering substantive questions.

### BRANCH T — "We're already building this ourselves" (read the Decision Card first)

**For most prospects worth this pipeline, this is the single most likely objection,
and it is usually the one named in Decision Card §5 as the primary decision risk.**
It arrives precisely *because* the video worked: a founder who is actively
systemising has, by definition, already started. Every strength the research
surfaced — the playbook, the scorecards, the new hires, the frameworks — is
simultaneously the reason they need you and the reason they'll say no.

It is emphatically **not** Branch H. Branch H is a rival vendor; Branch T is the
prospect's own pride, competence, and sunk effort. Arguing against those loses.

1. **Agree, completely and without reservation.** Any "yes, but" here reads as a
   salesman contradicting a CEO about her own company, and it is fatal.
2. **Never suggest what they built is wrong.** It isn't. Separate the *work* from
   its *format* — the same move as `p3b` Rule 7. Their thinking is right; a document
   simply cannot execute itself, and the person maintaining it is the constraint.
3. **Aim at the cost of doing it themselves — their time**, which is the one resource
   a founder cannot buy back and already knows is scarce.

> "You should be building it — nobody outside your group knows how it ought to work.
> The part I'd push on is that you're building it *by hand, personally,* and the
> whole point of it is to make the group run without you. That's the loop I'd want
> to close. The session isn't me proposing a replacement for your work — it's
> putting real numbers under it so you know which part is worth automating first."

If they hold: **do not push.** Leave the frame and exit like Branch D. This objection
frequently converts months later, when the internal build stalls exactly where you
said it would — and it will convert to *you*, because you predicted it without
gloating. Set a 90-day re-engagement.

### BRANCH U — The prospect volunteers internal numbers (the L1 upgrade — do not fumble this)

Occasionally a prospect answers Message 1 with real internal data: *"honestly, my
managers spend maybe ten hours a week on this."* This is the single highest-value
event in the entire pipeline and there is a strong instinct to do the wrong thing
with it.

- **Do not treat this as permission to compute the diagnosis for free.** Do not send
  back arithmetic. The moment you multiply his number into an annual cost and hand it
  over, you have delivered the consultation's headline deliverable in a DM and the
  gate is gone.
- **Do capture it.** It upgrades an L4/Excluded metric to L1. Record it verbatim,
  with attribution, for `p3d`.
- **Do use it as an anchor, not as a result.** Reflect the number back **unmultiplied**
  and let him do the arithmetic himself — his own number, his own conclusion, and no
  claim of yours he can dispute.
- **Do not update the video's stakes figure retroactively** in any message. The video
  said what it said (Alignment Rule). The new number is the reason for the session,
  not a correction of the past.

> "Ten hours a week, per manager, across five offices — that's the first number I
> couldn't see from outside, and it's bigger than I'd assumed. I'm not going to do
> that multiplication in a DM; doing it properly, with the rest of your numbers
> beside it, is exactly what the session is."

This is also the strongest possible transition into Message 2, because the anchor is
now **his own figure**, not yours. If Trap 2 applied and the video had no stakes
number, this branch supplies one — sourced entirely from him, which is better than
anything the research could have produced.

### BRANCH W — "How would I even pay you?" (cross-border payment friction)

Never treated as an objection, and it quietly kills deals. The legitimacy line
promises "a proper invoice" but never says *how*, and a US owner-operator paying an
overseas studio has real, unspoken questions: is this a wire, will my bookkeeper
accept it, is there a real entity behind it, what currency, what happens if it goes
wrong. Silence on this reads as amateurism at the precise moment professionalism
is being assessed.

**Have the answer ready before Message 2 is ever sent.** Name the mechanism, the
entity, and the currency in one plain sentence, with no defensiveness.

> "Invoice from Dev8X, payable by [card / bank transfer / the platform you use], in
> USD. You'll get a proper receipt for your books. And to be clear about the order of
> things — the invoice only exists after the session, and only if you tell me it was
> worth it."

The last sentence is the point. Under post-paid, payment mechanics are a **logistics
question, not a risk question**, and answering it that way removes the fear rather
than merely addressing the question. If the prospect raises payment mechanics
*before* the session, they have already decided to do the session.

### BRANCH X — "If you're guaranteeing it, why does it cost anything at all?"

A sharp buyer's question, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a
manoeuvre. The honest one is also the persuasive one.

> "Because free advice gets treated like free advice. If you're not paying anything,
> you'll take the call between two other things and the diagnosis will sit in your
> inbox. The fee is what makes us both show up properly — and it's the only reason I
> can afford to spend the time getting it right instead of getting it fast. If it
> turns out I didn't earn it, you don't pay it."

Do not smile through this one and do not soften it. It is a statement about how
serious work gets done, and a founder who runs a five-office group already knows it
is true. **Never** answer this by lowering the price — that concedes the question.

### BRANCH V — "This feels invasive" / "How do you know all this about me?"

Distinct from Branch N (which is a curiosity question about *who you are*). This one
is a *threat response*, and it is a live risk for any deeply-researched cold video.
Warmth loses here; only facts and a real exit restore safety.

1. **Do not apologise profusely, do not explain your methodology at length.** Both
   read as guilt.
2. **Name every source, specifically, in one line.** The entire defence is that a
   stranger with an afternoon could have found all of it.
3. **Hand back control immediately and mean it.**

> "Everything in that video came from things you published — your posts, your
> website. No tools, no data brokers, nothing private; I just read carefully and took
> it seriously. If it landed wrong, say the word and I'll delete the research and not
> contact you again."

Then **stop.** Do not attach the offer to this message, do not pivot to the
bottleneck, do not follow up in five days. If he replies, he has forgiven it and you
may continue. If he does not, honour the exit exactly as offered — and if he asked you
to delete the research, actually delete it.

### BRANCH Y — "Yes" (the branch nobody writes, and the one that leaks)

There is no scripted response to acceptance anywhere in this pipeline, which means the
most fragile moment in the whole sequence — after he has said yes, before he has sat
down — is improvised. **Between the yes and the session, nothing has been paid and
nothing has been delivered.** The prospect's enthusiasm is at its peak and decaying;
his calendar is not. Silent no-shows and quiet reschedules happen here, and they read
to Abdul as rejection when they are almost always just entropy.

**Reply within the hour if humanly possible.** Latency here is read as disorganisation
by someone who has just decided to trust you with an hour and, eventually, money.

The confirmation message does exactly four things:
1. **Two concrete time options, not a calendar link.** A link is a small delegation of
   work and it converts a warm yes into a task. Two options convert it into a reply.
2. **Name the deliverable again, as an object.** He is agreeing to leave with a thing.
3. **Ask him to bring the two or three numbers you confessed you couldn't see** — the
   exact ones from Beat 3. This is the single highest-leverage sentence in the
   message: it makes the session work, it makes him invest before it starts, and it
   quietly proves the video was not a performance.
4. **Restate the ceiling once, then never again.** He should walk in owing nothing and
   knowing it.

> "Good — [Tuesday 9am] or [Wednesday 2pm], whichever suits. It'll take about 45
> minutes and you'll leave with the written [named deliverable]. If you can, have
> [the two or three Beat 3 unknowns] roughly to hand — even ballpark. Nothing's owed
> until the end, and only if you say it was worth it."

**Do not send an invoice, a contract, a deposit request, or an intake form.** All four
are prepayment behaviours in disguise and all four contradict the guarantee he just
accepted. **Do not send a long pre-read.** He agreed to a session, not homework.

**If he goes quiet after saying yes:** one message, seven days later, containing no
pressure and no offer.
> "Still happy to do this whenever it suits — just say the word."
Then stop. A yes that decays is not a no; it is a busy person. It re-opens far more
often than a chased one does.

Hand off to `p3d_Consultation_Playbook.md` the moment a time is agreed.

### The unanswered-message counter (the stop rule needs something to count)

"Three total unanswered messages across all branches" cannot be enforced without a
tally, and branch follow-ups (K, P, Message 3, Branch B, Branch C) each add one
without any of them knowing about the others. **Maintain an explicit count in the
prospect's tracking row, and increment it for every message sent that receives no
reply.** At three, stop — regardless of which branch you are in and regardless of
how promising the last message felt. Reset to zero on any reply.

### Ownership of the not-watched path (resolve the overlap with `p3b`)

Two files currently claim this prospect. `p3b` Part 0.1 authorises a **second wrapper
message** if the video is unwatched after 5–7 days; Branch C here authorises a
**not-watched touch** at 5–7 days. These are the same message, and sending both
produces two nudges about an unwatched video inside a week — the exact pressure
pattern this playbook forbids everywhere else.

**Ruling: `p3b` owns everything up to the first watch.** The second wrapper is `p3b`'s
and it is the only not-watched touch permitted. Branch C exists here solely to
document the wording for the operator; it is **not an additional send.** If the second
wrapper has already gone out, Branch C is spent — stop and go to 90-day re-engagement.
`p3c` owns the thread only from the moment the video is watched or replied to.

**The universal rule:** if continuing feels like chasing, stop. Pressure
destroys the relationship for any future contact. Dev8X's edge is depth and
trust — not persistence and volume.

## CONFIDENCE SIGNAL ADJUSTMENTS

| Signal | Message 2 fee |
|--------|----------------|
| **High** | Ceiling frame, stated once, no softening. Band per complexity. |
| **Medium** | Same ceiling frame, same band — **do not discount for uncertainty.** What flexes is the *diagnosis* ("the estimate firms up together"), never the price. |
| **Low** | Do NOT push the full paid ask — Message 1 does MORE qualification, and Message 2 (if reached) leads with the rigor of the method and the guarantee rather than a confident diagnosis. |

**Confidence never moves the number.** A lower fee does not compensate a shakier
diagnosis; it advertises one. The Confidence Signal governs how firmly the *diagnosis*
is asserted, and the guarantee — not the price — is what carries a Medium-confidence
offer. A seller who discounts because he is unsure has told the buyer he is unsure and
charged him for the privilege of noticing.

At Low confidence, when in genuine doubt, gather more before charging — pushing
a paid offer on thin research is where confident wrongness does the most
damage.
