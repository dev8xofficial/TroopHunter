## 2 — What doing your own credentialing really costs

url: https://www.piehealthusa.com/what-doing-your-own-credentialing-really-costs/
type: article
meta: On paper, credentialing looks like a way to save money. A practice manager, office admin, or clinician owner handles the forms, makes the calls, and you avoid paying a service fee. Once you put numbers to the time involved, that story changes.

### Start with the real hourly cost of your team

On paper, credentialing looks like a way to save money. Once you put numbers to the time involved, that story changes.

In most small practices, the people who end up doing credentialing are: the practice manager or operations lead, an office administrator who already has a full plate, or a clinician owner who splits time between clients and admin.

You can estimate the cost in two ways:
- For managers and admins: fully loaded hourly rate, often $30–$50/hr including benefits and taxes.
- For clinicians: collected revenue per clinical hour, often $80–$150/session for behavioral health.

### How much time does one enrollment really take

A single clean enrollment for one provider with one payor involves: researching requirements, gathering documents, completing the application, responding to questions, following up when the file goes quiet, and confirming approval and directory listing.

For someone who does not do this daily, this easily adds up to 6–10 hours of scattered work per enrollment over a couple of months. A middle estimate of 8 hours per enrollment is a realistic working assumption.

### Example 1: practice manager time

A practice manager at $45/hr, 8 hours per enrollment, 6 payors for one provider:
- 8 × 6 = 48 hours × $45 = **$2,160** of internal cost for that one provider.
- For two providers: **$4,320**.

### Example 2: behavioral health group owner

A behavioral health group owner collecting $120/session, 5 clients/day, pulls back just one day per month to do credentialing:
- 5 × $120 = **$600 revenue per day**
- Over 6 months of a launch: **$3,600 of clinical revenue traded for admin work**

### Multiply by the number of payors and providers

2 providers × 6 payors × 8 hours = **96 hours of credentialing time**.
- At $40/hr: **$3,840**
- At $50/hr: **$4,800**

### Compare that to the cost of a service

Credentialing partners usually price per enrollment, per provider for a set of payors, or as a monthly service. The all-in cost to have a specialist handle one provider's core enrollments is usually less than several thousand dollars of internal labor, especially at scale.

### The cost of delays is even bigger

One therapist or NP seeing 6 patients/day at $100/visit generates ~$600/day or ~$12,000 over four weeks. If avoidable credentialing mistakes push the effective date out by one extra month, that is roughly **$12,000 of potential revenue at risk** for that provider.

### The break-even point

Key questions to sketch:
- Hourly cost of your manager/admin
- Hours spent per enrollment
- Number of payors per provider
- Number of providers to credential this year
- Rough collected revenue per clinical day

If the service cost is lower than the time and revenue already being spent—or if it prevents even one month of avoidable delay—the math favors outsourcing.

## 3 — Let Credentialing Be Someone Else's Full Time Job

url: https://www.piehealthusa.com/let-credentialing-be-someone-elses-full-time-job/
type: article
meta: If you are a practice owner or manager, you already have a full time job.

### The hidden cost of learning credentialing

To really learn credentialing well, a practice owner would need to: understand the difference between credentialing, enrollment, contracting, and directories; learn specific rules for each commercial, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid managed care, and specialty network; keep track of portals (CAQH, Availity, plan-specific sites); and stay current on changing requirements.

### What you are not doing while you "save money"

Every hour spent chasing status updates or fixing application errors is an hour not spent on: refining schedule templates, improving intake processes, coaching staff, or building referral relationships. None of those tasks can be fully outsourced. Credentialing can.

### Credentialing is unforgiving of beginners

Common painful patterns:
- Applications sent with small inconsistencies causing quiet delays
- CAQH profiles left unattested, stalling multiple payors at once
- A new provider added to the schedule before enrollments are complete
- A recredentialing notice missed, leading to surprise network termination

### Depth beats occasional attention

Credentialing rewards depth, repetition, and systems. Teams who do it every day: know where errors usually show up; have checklists and templates; keep a clean source of truth for provider data; maintain a follow-up rhythm.

### What you actually want to own

Practices should own the decisions: which patient populations to serve, which major payors are essential, what payer mix is comfortable, and how fast revenue needs to start. The mechanics of executing those decisions do not need direct owner time.

### Buying back your time

If you add up all hours spent researching requirements, filling out forms, sitting on hold, and fixing denials, that number is not small. The question: if you had all those hours back this year, where would you put them?

## 4 — What happens after you're credentialed

url: https://www.piehealthusa.com/what-happens-after-youre-credentialed/
type: article
meta: Getting credentialed feels like the finish line. But credentialing is not the end of the work. It is the beginning of an ongoing maintenance cycle.

### Recredentialing

Every payor recredentials participating providers on a regular cycle, typically every 2–3 years (some annually). Recredentialing re-verifies licenses, malpractice, board certifications, work history, and disciplinary actions. Missing a deadline can result in network removal; reinstatement is harder than staying current.

### License and certification renewals

State medical license, DEA registration, malpractice insurance, and board certifications all have expiration dates. Most payors require these to stay current. A brief lapse can trigger a credentialing hold or network termination.

### CAQH profile maintenance

Most commercial payors use CAQH ProView as a central data source. Profiles must be re-attested every **120 days**. A lapsed CAQH attestation does not affect just one payor — because many plans pull from CAQH, a stale profile creates downstream problems across the entire network simultaneously.

### Roster and directory updates

If practice information changes (new address, tax ID, group NPI, added locations), those changes must be reported to every payor. Directory errors are common and have real consequences: patients call to verify in-network status, get incorrect information, and either do not book or receive surprise out-of-network bills.

### Adding providers to an existing group

A new provider does not inherit the group's payor relationships automatically. Each new provider must be enrolled individually with each payor under the group's contracts. Best practice: begin credentialing the moment an offer is accepted, not after the provider starts seeing patients.

### Terminating payor relationships

Most payor contracts require formal written notice of termination, typically 60–90 days in advance. Failure to follow the process correctly can create liability and delay joining a different plan later.

### What ongoing credentialing management actually looks like

Staying current across multiple providers and payors requires: a calendar of all expiration dates, regular CAQH attestation on schedule, recredentialing tracking by payor, roster management, directory audits, and documented termination processes.

## 5 — How we build a payor launch plan

url: https://www.piehealthusa.com/how-we-build-a-payor-launch-plan/
type: article
meta: When a new practice opens or a provider joins a group, one of the first real decisions is: which insurance plans do we actually enroll with?

### Start with your patient population

Before looking at any payor, start with who the practice is actually trying to serve. Geography matters — dominant plans in Georgetown, TX differ from Houston or Austin. Specialty matters — a behavioral health practice has a very different payor mix than primary care.

Key question: who is the intended patient, where do they live, and what insurance do they most likely carry?

### Map the local payor landscape

Map plans with meaningful market share in that area based on actual enrollment and claims data — not just name recognition. Which plans do employers in the region most commonly offer? Which Medicaid managed care organizations are active? Which Medicare Advantage plans have high penetration? A handful of plans typically account for the majority of covered lives.

### Sequence for speed and cash flow

Not all payor enrollments take the same time. Some commercial plans credential within 60 days; others (especially certain Medicaid MCOs or closed-network plans) take 4–6 months or longer. Build the sequence with cash flow in mind: faster-moving plans go in first so revenue is flowing while longer processes are still in motion.

### Account for provider type and specialty

- Physicians (MD/DO) typically move through commercial credentialing more quickly.
- NPs and PAs face additional scrutiny around supervising physician requirements.
- Behavioral health providers often find participation more restricted and waitlists for certain networks are closed entirely.
- Dental and vision providers operate in separate networks with their own pathways and timelines.

### Flag closed networks and alternatives

Check network status before applications go out. When a preferred network is closed, identify alternatives: other plans serving the same population, gap exception processes, or a timeline for when the network may reopen.

### Build a tracking system, not just a list

A payor launch plan is a living tracker — applications go out, statuses change, effective dates get confirmed, directories need verification. Every step has a follow-up, a deadline, and an owner.

### The result

A new practice ends with: a prioritized list of payor targets, a realistic timeline for when each enrollment is likely active, an understanding of which plans may take longer, and a clear picture of what cash flow will look like in the first 6–12 months.

## 6 — From credentialing to in network: how it all connects

url: https://www.piehealthusa.com/from-credentialing-to-in-network-how-it-all-connects/
type: article
meta: Most people use the word "credentialing" to mean all of it. In practice, those are separate steps.

### Credentialing

The process of verifying who a provider is — education, training, licenses, and work history are accurate and current. Credentialing answers: **is this provider qualified and compliant?**

### Enrollment

The process of getting a provider or practice officially registered with a specific payor or plan (also called payor enrollment or provider enrollment). A provider can be credentialed and still not enrolled with a specific payor. Both must happen before claims will be paid. Enrollment answers: **is this provider officially registered with this plan?**

### Contracting

The agreement between practice and payor setting the terms of participation: covered services, reimbursement rates, billing rules, and compliance requirements. In many cases contracting is tied to enrollment; in others (larger groups, multi-specialty practices) contracts may be negotiated separately. Contracting answers: **under what terms are we participating?**

### Directories and effective dates

Once credentialing, enrollment, and contracting are complete, the payor updates their provider directory. Directory updates do not always happen immediately — there can be a lag of days or weeks between approval and accurate directory listing.

The **effective date** is the date the payor officially recognizes participation. Services before that date are generally not covered under that plan, regardless of when the application was submitted or approved.

### Why this matters for planning

A delay in any one step holds up everything that follows. The real answer to "are we in network?" is: "credentialed with this payor, enrolled under this plan, contracted under these terms, and listed correctly in this directory."

### One brand, many plans

Most major insurance companies are not a single network. Under one brand exist employer plans, marketplace plans, Medicare Advantage plans, and Medicaid managed care plans — each with its own credentialing requirements, enrollment pathways, and contracting terms. Being enrolled with UnitedHealthcare commercial does not automatically mean enrollment in their Medicare Advantage or Medicaid products.

## 7 — How long does credentialing really take?

url: https://www.piehealthusa.com/how-long-does-credentialing-really-take/
type: article
meta: Most practices hear "about 90 days" and plan around that. In real life, the timeline depends on which payors you are working with, how complete your information is, and where you are starting from.

### The clock does not start when you think it does

The timeline most payors quote begins when they receive a **clean, complete application** — not when you start gathering documents. Getting from "we need to credential" to "applications are submitted" usually takes 2–4 weeks. So when someone says "60 days":
- 2–4 weeks of prep
- Plus the payor's processing time (in business days, not calendar days)
- Plus back-and-forth on missing information

That 60-day window realistically becomes 3–4 months on the calendar before a provider is approved and billing.

### What "business days" actually means

A quoted 90 business-day window translates to roughly **18 calendar weeks** (~4.5 months) before factoring in any delays.

### Realistic timelines by payor type

Ranges vary; clean submissions with complete information land on the shorter end. Commercial plans: commonly 60–120 days. Medicare/PECOS: ~15 days for electronic submissions without site visits; up to 50 days with site visit. Medicaid: 45–90 days (federal standard, though many states miss these). Medicare Advantage / Medicaid MCO: add another 60–90 days after primary enrollment.

### Starting new vs. transitioning from another practice

- **Starting from scratch**: near the longer end of every range; no existing enrollment history.
- **Transitioning from another practice**: prior enrollments do not automatically transfer. Each payor treats a new tax ID as a new application.
- **Adding a new provider to an existing practice**: usually faster, but still requires individual applications for each payor and provider.

### Effective dates and what that means for billing

- **Medicare**: allows retroactive effective dates up to 30 days before approval if application was submitted cleanly.
- **Medicaid**: retroactive billing rules vary by state.
- **Most commercial payors**: do not offer retroactive effective dates. Visits before the effective date are typically not reimbursable.

### What adds time

- Starting after a provider is already seeing patients
- Incomplete or inconsistent information across applications and payor profiles
- Submitting through the wrong product or pathway
- Outdated licenses, malpractice coverage, or CAQH profiles
- Slow responses to payor questions
- Panel closures requiring a waitlist or separate process

### How to plan against real timelines

- Treat 90 days as the minimum, not the target
- Count in calendar weeks, not business days
- Start 4–6 months before a provider needs to be billing
- Build in time for at least one round of back-and-forth with each payor
- Do not assume approvals will arrive simultaneously for all payors

## 10 — How to Join Insurance Panels

url: https://www.piehealthusa.com/how-to-join-insurance-panels/
type: article
meta: Learn how to join insurance panels, what paneling means, and how credentialing, enrollment, and contracting work together.

### What Does Insurance Paneling Mean?

Insurance paneling = becoming an in-network provider. When on a panel, a provider is listed as participating and can bill for services at contracted rates. Being "on a panel" means credentialed, contracted, assigned an effective date, and loaded into the payer system so claims can be paid.

### Credentialing vs Enrollment vs Contracting vs Paneling

- **Credentialing**: payer reviews your qualifications
- **Enrollment**: you are added to the payer's system
- **Contracting**: you sign an agreement with rates and effective date
- **Paneling**: the result of ALL of the above being completed

A provider may be credentialed but not yet contracted, or contracted but not yet loaded — in those cases, they are not fully in-network and not billable.

### Steps to Join Insurance Panels

1. Complete and maintain your CAQH profile
2. Submit credentialing applications to payers
3. Payer reviews and verifies your information
4. Credentialing is approved
5. Contract is issued and signed
6. Provider is loaded into the payer system
7. Effective date is assigned

The effective date — not your start date or application date — determines when billing can begin.

### Choosing Which Insurance Panels to Join

Factors: reimbursement rates, patient population, demand in your area, administrative requirements. Being approved to join a panel does not mean the contract is financially favorable.

### Documents Required for Panel Applications

Most pulled from CAQH: state licenses, DEA certificate (if applicable), board certifications, malpractice insurance, work history, education and training. Inconsistencies between the application and CAQH can delay approval.

### Panel Approval Timelines

Quoted at 90–120 days; in practice often 3–6 months or more before becoming fully billable.

### Maintaining Participation in Insurance Panels

Ongoing maintenance required: keeping CAQH updated and active, re-attesting every 120 days, updating licenses and documents, maintaining compliance with payer requirements.

## 11 — How Long Insurance Credentialing Takes

url: https://www.piehealthusa.com/how-long-insurance-credentialing-takes/

### How Long Does Insurance Credentialing Take?

Most insurance companies quote **90–120 days**, measured in business days, beginning only after a complete application is submitted. In real-world conditions, it is common for credentialing to take **3–6 months or longer** before a provider can reliably bill insurance.

### Credentialing vs Enrollment Timelines

A provider is not billable when credentialing is approved. A provider becomes billable only after: the contract is signed, an effective date is issued, and the provider is loaded into the payer system. The payer's effective date controls when claims can be paid.

### Why Credentialing Can Take Months

Credentialing involves multiple steps and multiple parties: review of CAQH data, primary source verification, internal payer processing, contracting, and system loading. Even when everything is done correctly, payer processing timelines are outside the provider's control.

### Factors That Affect Credentialing Speed

- Completeness of the application
- Status of CAQH profile
- Payer backlog
- Specialty demand
- Responsiveness to requests

### Common Delays

- Inactive or incomplete CAQH profiles
- Missing or expired documents
- Data inconsistencies
- Payer delays or lost applications

### The Hiring and Revenue Impact

Practices often hire providers based on a planned start date without aligning credentialing timelines. If credentialing is not started at least 90–120 days before a provider's start date, there is a high likelihood that provider will not be billable for months — creating a period where the practice is paying salary without generating insurance revenue.

### How Practices Reduce Credentialing Delays

- Starting credentialing early
- Maintaining accurate CAQH profiles
- Submitting complete applications
- Responding quickly to payer requests

## 12 — Insurance Credentialing: How the Credentialing Process Works

### What Is Insurance Credentialing?

Insurance credentialing is the process insurance companies use to verify a provider's qualifications and approve them to participate in a network. Before a provider can bill insurance, the payer must review their education, training, licensure, and work history. Much of this information is pulled directly from CAQH.

Credentialing is not just administrative — it determines whether and when a provider can generate revenue.

### Credentialing vs Enrollment vs Contracting

- **Credentialing**: review of qualifications
- **Enrollment**: adding to the payer's system so claims can be processed
- **Contracting**: signing an agreement with reimbursement rates and effective date

A provider is not billable until all three are complete and the payer assigns an effective date and loads the provider into their system.

### Why Providers Must Credential With Insurance Companies

- Claims cannot be paid without credentialing
- Providers are not considered in-network
- The practice cannot generate insurance revenue

Credentialing is directly tied to revenue, not just compliance.

### The Role of CAQH in Credentialing

CAQH is the primary data source used by payers during credentialing. Payers pull provider data directly from CAQH and expect it to be accurate and current. If the CAQH profile is outdated or inconsistent, the application can be delayed or pended. Errors in CAQH follow into every credentialing application.

### Documents Required for Credentialing

Consistent documentation required: state licenses, DEA certificate (if applicable), board certifications, malpractice insurance, work history, education and training. Any mismatch between CAQH and submitted applications can trigger delays.

### Steps in the Credentialing Process

1. Complete or update CAQH profile
2. Submit application to the payer
3. Payer reviews CAQH and documentation
4. Primary source verification is completed
5. Application is approved or pended
6. Contract is issued and signed
7. Provider is loaded into the payer system
8. Effective date is assigned

### Credentialing and Hiring: The Costly Gap

Providers are often hired with a planned start date, but credentialing is not aligned with that timeline. If credentialing is not started at least 90–120 days in advance, there is a strong chance the provider will not be billable for months. This gap — paying salary without generating insurance revenue — is one of the most common and expensive mistakes growing practices make.

## 13 — CAQH Profile Guide: How to Complete Your CAQH Profile

url: https://www.piehealthusa.com/caqh-profile-guide-how-to-complete-your-caqh-profile/
type: article
meta: Learn how to complete your CAQH profile, register, and avoid common mistakes that delay credentialing and insurance payments.

### What Is a CAQH Profile?

Your CAQH profile is the centralized record insurance companies use to evaluate you for credentialing and enrollment. It includes: personal and practice information, education and training, work history, licenses and certifications, malpractice insurance, and supporting documents. When you apply to a payer, they pull your data directly from CAQH — so the profile must be accurate, complete, and current at all times.

### Documents Required for CAQH

- State licenses
- DEA certificate (if applicable)
- Board certifications
- Malpractice insurance certificate
- Work history details
- Education and training records
- Practice location information

### How to Register for CAQH

Register through CAQH ProView. Create an account, enter basic provider information, receive your CAQH ID, begin completing your profile.

### Step-by-Step CAQH Profile Setup

Sections to complete: personal and demographic information, professional history, licensure and certifications, practice locations, malpractice coverage. Each section must be complete and consistent. Small inconsistencies (mismatched dates, missing details) can trigger delays.

### Attesting Your CAQH Profile

Once the profile is complete, you must attest to it to confirm the information is accurate. CAQH requires re-attestation every **120 days**. If you do not re-attest: your profile becomes inactive, payers may stop processing applications, credentialing can be delayed, and claims may be affected.

### Granting Payer Access to Your Profile

Completing your profile is not enough — you must also grant access to each payer by selecting the payer in CAQH and authorizing them to view your profile. If access is not granted, the payer cannot review your application even if everything else is complete.

### Updating and Maintaining Your CAQH Profile

- Re-attest every 120 days
- Update licenses and documents before they expire
- Keep work history current
- Ensure consistency across all sections

### Common CAQH Errors That Delay Credentialing

- Missing or expired documents
- Inconsistent information across sections
- Gaps in work history
- Failure to re-attest every 120 days
- Not granting payer access
- Profiles not reviewed in months

### Why CAQH Maintenance Matters More Than Setup

### What New York Providers Actually Need to Know

New York is one of the most structurally complex credentialing environments in the country. Licensure is handled by the **New York State Education Department (NYSED)** rather than a department of health. Medicaid enrollment runs through **eMedNY**. Managed care plans require separate credentialing. New York imposes unique Medicaid compliance obligations through the **Office of the Medicaid Inspector General (OMIG)**.

### New York State Level Prerequisites

NYSED licenses more than 60 professions; a professional license is valid for life unless revoked, but **registration to practice must be renewed** (physicians: every 2 years; most other professions: every 3 years). Payors verify **active registration status**, not just the existence of a license. A lapsed registration will fail credentialing verification even though the underlying license technically remains valid.

NYSED assigns each profession a two-digit Profession Code. When enrolling in Medicaid through eMedNY, providers must enter a **three-digit Profession Code** (add a leading zero to the two-digit code and place it before the license number). Incorrect Profession Code prefix is a frequent cause of eMedNY enrollment rejection.

### New York Medicaid Enrollment Through eMedNY

New York Medicaid enrollment is administered by the Department of Health through the eMedNY system. Under the 21st Century Cures Act, all providers participating in any New York Medicaid MCO network must be screened and enrolled through eMedNY even if they never bill fee-for-service Medicaid directly.

New York remains **heavily paper-based** for Medicaid enrollment. Providers must locate the correct application package for their provider type, complete the paper application, include required documentation, and mail it. For clean and complete applications, eMedNY enrollment commonly takes **60–90 days**.

Upon approval, providers receive a Medicaid Provider ID and an effective date of enrollment. For electronic claims submission, providers must also obtain an Electronic Transmitter Identification Number (ETAN).

### New York Medicaid Managed Care

New York has one of the most fragmented Medicaid managed care landscapes, with numerous regional and statewide plans. Each MCO maintains its own credentialing committee, documentation requirements, and network capacity decisions. Some MCOs may provisionally credential providers while eMedNY enrollment is pending. **MCO credentialing generally requires an additional 60–90 days** after eMedNY enrollment.

### OMIG Compliance and Its Credentialing Impact

New York is the only state with a standalone OMIG that imposes mandatory compliance program requirements tied to Medicaid participation. Any Medicaid provider that claims, orders, or receives at least **$1 million in Medicaid payments** in any consecutive 12-month period must adopt, implement, and maintain an effective compliance program. Providers must certify annually by December 31. Some MCOs verify OMIG compliance status during credentialing and recredentialing.

### Commercial Credentialing in New York

New York's commercial market is divided between downstate and upstate:
- **Empire BlueCross BlueShield**: New York City, Long Island, Westchester, Hudson Valley
- **Excellus BlueCross BlueShield**: Rochester, Syracuse, other upstate regions
- **Highmark BlueCross BlueShield of Western New York**: Buffalo region
- **EmblemHealth**: major NYC-based carrier with strong municipal and union enrollment
- **Healthfirst, Fidelis Care**: significant Medicaid and exchange footprints
- **Upstate regional plans**: CDPHP, MVP Health Care, Independent Health

Credentialing with one Blue Cross affiliate does NOT provide network access with another.

### Typical Credentialing Timelines in New York

- NYSED license processing: 3–6 months once documentation is complete
- eMedNY Medicaid enrollment: 60–90 days for clean paper applications
- Medicaid Managed Care credentialing: additional 60–90 days after eMedNY
- Commercial plan credentialing: 60–120 days
- Medicare enrollment (PECOS): 60–90 days

### Common Credentialing Mistakes in New York

- Confusing NYSED licensure with active registration
- Using incorrect Profession Code prefixes in eMedNY applications
- Assuming eMedNY enrollment covers managed care credentialing
- Credentialing with only one Blue Cross affiliate despite serving patients across multiple regions
- Ignoring OMIG compliance obligations
- Submitting applications before licenses are fully active or registered

### What Florida Providers Actually Need to Know

Florida's credentialing landscape is shaped by three structural realities: Medicaid enrollment through AHCA with mandatory **Level 2 background screening**; nearly all Medicaid beneficiaries in managed care under the **Statewide Medicaid Managed Care (SMMC)** program; and a commercial market heavily concentrated with **Florida Blue (GuideWell)**, UnitedHealthcare, and Aetna.

### Florida State-Level Prerequisites

All healthcare professional licensing in Florida is administered by the **Florida Department of Health (DOH), Division of Medical Quality Assurance**. Effective July 1, 2025, Florida expanded background screening requirements — providers in screened professions must complete fingerprint-based state and federal screening at their next licensure renewal.

### Florida Medicaid Enrollment: AHCA and FLMMIS

Florida Medicaid enrollment is administered by AHCA through the **Florida Medicaid Management Information System (FLMMIS)**. Two enrollment paths:
- **Full Enrollment**: for providers who will bill Medicaid fee-for-service directly
- **Limited Enrollment**: for providers who participate only through Medicaid managed care plans

Both require Level 2 background screening. The Florida enrollment structure: individual providers enroll under a single Medicaid Provider ID; group practices and facilities enroll separately; individual rendering providers must be properly **linked to the group enrollment** for claims to process correctly. Failing to complete group-to-individual linking is a common cause of claim denials.

### Level 2 Background Screening

AHCA requires Level 2 fingerprint-based background screening for all individuals listed on a Medicaid enrollment application (all owners with 5%+ ownership, managing employees, board members). Providers must first submit their Medicaid enrollment application and receive an **Application Tracking Number** before initiating fingerprinting. Fingerprinting delays are one of the most common enrollment bottlenecks in Florida.

### SMMC and Provider Master List Verification

Under SMMC, managed care plans must verify that providers appear as **Active on AHCA's Provider Master List** before claims can be paid — even if the provider has executed a contract with a managed care plan.

### Commercial Credentialing in Florida

Florida Blue is the top commercial priority for most practices due to dominant statewide market share. Other key plans: AvMed (South Florida), Ambetter (ACA exchange), Humana and UnitedHealthcare (Medicare Advantage). Panel closures are common in major metro areas.

### Typical Credentialing Timelines in Florida

- DOH license processing: varies by profession
- FLMMIS enrollment: 45–90 days (including Level 2 screening)
- Managed care and commercial plan credentialing: 60–120 days

### Common Credentialing Mistakes in Florida

- Delaying Level 2 background screening after Medicaid application submission
- Providing incorrect addresses for fingerprint-based screening correspondence
- Submitting applications before licenses are fully active
- Assuming Medicaid enrollment automatically creates managed care participation
- Not verifying Active status before billing Medicaid claims
- Incomplete group-to-individual linking in Medicaid enrollment
- Billing insurance before effective dates are confirmed

### What South Carolina Providers Actually Need to Know

South Carolina's credentialing landscape has several structural complexities: licensing is managed by LLR but controlled substance registration by a completely separate agency (**South Carolina Department of Public Health, DPH**); Medicaid enrollment flows through SCDHHS into a **five-MCO managed care system**; the commercial side is dominated by BlueCross BlueShield of South Carolina; and in **November 2024** the state halted enrollment of any new Medicaid managed care plans, locking the current MCO structure in place.

FCVS processing alone can take 30–45 days; total timelines for physician licenses often reach **4–6 months** when FCVS is factored in.

Mid-level providers face collaborative/supervision requirements: APRNs must maintain a written collaborative practice agreement with a physician; PAs must practice under physician supervision. Missing or outdated agreements can stall applications.

### South Carolina Controlled Substance Registration

South Carolina requires a **state controlled substance registration before a federal DEA registration** — the reverse of many states. Key details:
- Administered by the Department of Public Health, Bureau of Drug Control

- Renewal deadlines differ by provider type: MDs/DOs/EMS renew October 1; all others renew April 1
- For APRNs and PAs, the DPH application requires a **wet signature** from the collaborating/supervising physician — scanned or electronic signatures are not accepted

### South Carolina Medicaid Enrollment

SCDHHS enrollment through the online provider enrollment portal. Applications must be completed and submitted within **30 days** (incomplete applications are purged after 30 days and must be restarted). For clean applications, SCDHHS enrollment typically takes **30–60 days**.

### South Carolina Medicaid Managed Care: Healthy Connections

Five MCOs operate statewide (locked since November 2024): Absolute Total Care, Healthy Blue, Select Health of South Carolina, Molina Healthcare of South Carolina, Humana Healthy Horizons in South Carolina. SCDHHS enrollment is only the first step; each MCO manages its own credentialing and contracting independently. MCO credentialing timelines: **60–90 days or longer** after SCDHHS enrollment.

### Commercial Credentialing in South Carolina

**BlueCross BlueShield of South Carolina (BCBSSC)** holds a dominant position across employer, individual, and Federal Employee Health Benefits markets. BCBSSC commercial products are separate from Healthy Blue Medicaid. **UnitedHealthcare entered the South Carolina ACA marketplace in 2026**.

### Common Credentialing Mistakes in South Carolina

- Applying for DEA before obtaining DPH controlled substance registration
- Letting DPH registration lapse (no grace period)
- Delaying FCVS initiation before LLR physician application
- Failing to complete SCDHHS applications within the 30-day window

- Missing wet signatures on mid-level DPH applications
- Confusing BCBSSC commercial participation with Healthy Blue Medicaid

Michigan credentialing runs through a state-specific Medicaid enrollment system called **CHAMPS**, a licensing framework administered by **LARA**, a managed care landscape requiring plan-by-plan credentialing, and a distinct behavioral health structure involving **PIHPs and CMHSPs**.

### Michigan State-Level Prerequisites

All professional licensing is managed by the **Michigan Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs (LARA)**, Bureau of Professional Licensing. LARA regulates 26 health professions under the Michigan Public Health Code.

Michigan requires a state-level **controlled substance license** in addition to a federal DEA registration. For APRNs, Michigan does not issue a controlled substance license to APRNs — instead, APRNs must have a written delegation agreement from a physician, and prescriptions must include both the APRN's and the delegating physician's names and DEA numbers.

### Medicaid Enrollment in Michigan: CHAMPS Is Mandatory

Michigan Medicaid enrollment is handled through the **Community Health Automated Medicaid Processing System (CHAMPS)**, managed by MDHHS.

Since January 1, 2018, **all providers who serve Michigan Medicaid beneficiaries** — including those who only participate in Medicaid MCO networks — must be screened and enrolled in CHAMPS (federal screening mandate). Enrolling in CHAMPS does not require a provider to accept fee-for-service Medicaid patients. CHAMPS approval typically takes **30–60 days**. All Michigan Medicaid providers must revalidate at least once every **five years** and report changes within **35 days**.

### Michigan Medicaid Managed Care: Enrollment Is Not Network Participation

Most Michigan Medicaid beneficiaries receive services through Medicaid Health Plans: Meridian, Molina, HAP, Blue Cross Complete of Michigan, Priority Health Choice, UnitedHealthcare Community Plan, Aetna Better Health, AmeriHealth Caritas, and Upper Peninsula Health Plan.

Two steps required: enroll in CHAMPS, then credential and contract with each Medicaid Health Plan separately. Michigan has a **Medicaid Common Credentialing Initiative** underway through the Michigan Association of Health Plans aiming to select a single CVO for Medicaid Health Plans in the Lower Peninsula.

### Behavioral Health Credentialing: PIHP and CMHSP Structure

Behavioral health providers must enroll in CHAMPS AND credential through the local **PIHP or CMHSP system** — these are distinct processes. PIHPs are required to use a uniform credentialing program; recredentialing is required every three years. Beginning **October 2025**, qualified mental health providers participating in Michigan Medicaid must incorporate the **MichiCANS Screener** (patients under 21) and **LOCUS tool** (adults).

### Commercial Credentialing in Michigan: BCBSM's Half-State Dominance

**BCBSM / Blue Care Network** together cover approximately **50% of Michigan's insurable population** — the highest market dominance of any regional insurer in the country.

- **BCBSM / Blue Care Network**: ~50% market share; 4.9 million members nationally; BCN is the HMO subsidiary; BCBSM and BCN require separate credentialing
- **Priority Health (Corewell Health subsidiary)**: ~13% market share; strongest in West Michigan
- **UnitedHealthcare**: ~6% market share
- **Meridian Health Plan (Centene)**: ~6% market share; primarily Medicaid
- **Molina Healthcare of Michigan**: ~8% (Wayne County)
- **HAP (Henry Ford Health)**: concentrated in Southeast Michigan (Detroit metro)
- **Physicians Health Plan**: regional plan in mid-Michigan (Lansing area)
- **McLaren Health Plan**: tied to McLaren Health system; select regions

- CHAMPS Medicaid enrollment: 30–60 days
- Medicaid Health Plan credentialing: 60–90 days after CHAMPS enrollment
- PIHP/CMHSP credentialing for behavioral health: 30–90 days
- Commercial plan credentialing: 60–120 days

## 31 — Provider Directory Accuracy: Why "Approved" Providers Still Lose Access, Claims, and Revenue

url: https://www.piehealthusa.com/provider-directory-accuracy-why-approved-providers-still-lose-access-claims-and-revenue/
type: article
meta: none

### What provider directory accuracy actually means

Provider directories contain: provider name and NPI, practice locations, specialties and taxonomy codes, network participation status, accepting-new-patients indicators, and contact information — sourced from CAQH, NPI registry, credentialing applications, roster submissions, and periodic attestations. Problems arise when those systems fall out of alignment. Joint work by the **American Medical Association and CAQH** found that **40–50% of provider directory listings contain at least one inaccuracy**, most often related to address, specialty, or network status.

### The legal framework

Under the ACA, CMS regulations for Medicare Advantage and Medicaid managed care, and the No Surprises Act, health plans are legally required to maintain accurate, up-to-date provider directories. **CMS requires Medicare Advantage and Medicaid managed care plans to verify directory information at least every 90 days**. NCQA accreditation standards require ongoing monitoring. The legal obligation sits with the payor — but the operational consequences are often felt by the practice.

### How inaccurate directory data affects providers in practice

When directory data is wrong or unverified, providers experience: claims denying or routing for manual review, prior authorizations misrouting or failing, eligibility systems treating the provider as out-of-network, providers marked as "not accepting new patients," and patients told the provider is inactive.

### Where directory accuracy breaks down most often

- **Addresses**: small differences (suite numbers, abbreviations, legacy locations) trigger mismatches
- **Specialty and taxonomy errors**: listing a general taxonomy when billing under a subspecialty
- **Network and location linking failures**: provider approved but not associated with the correct group contract, location, or delegated network
- **Provider status errors**: new providers showing as inactive or terminated providers remaining active
- **Accepting new patients indicators**: defaulting incorrectly when verification requests are missed

### Preparing for 2026

Practices should: assign responsibility for directory maintenance, ensure provider data aligns across systems, confirm how credentialing support is scoped, and track verification requests centrally. Directory issues are easier to prevent than to diagnose. Once they affect billing or access, identifying the cause and correcting it can take months.

## 39 — Provider Credentialing Requirements: Documents That Cause Delays

url: https://www.piehealthusa.com/provider-credentialing-requirements-documents-that-cause-delays-2/
type: article
meta: Enrolling with insurance payors should be straightforward, but small mistakes in your paperwork can cause massive delays.

### Your CP-575 Letter: Why It's Critical

When your business first registered for an EIN, the IRS sent a **CP-575 letter** confirming it. Payors use it to verify your business's legal name and tax ID. If the name or EIN on your CP-575 doesn't match exactly with other documents — even minor differences like a missing comma or an extra space — the application may be rejected.

If you don't have a copy, you must request a **147C Letter** from the IRS by calling 1-800-829-4933. This process takes **4–6 weeks**, so request it as soon as possible.

### W-9 Form: Do Your EIN and Business Name Match?

Payors compare your W-9 to your CP-575. If the business name or EIN does not match **exactly**, your application may be rejected. Ensure your W-9 reflects the exact business name and EIN from your CP-575.

### NPI Confirmation: Is Your Business Name Up to Date?

Payors verify that your NPI details match your tax records before processing enrollment. Log in to NPPES and confirm that your NPI details match your CP-575 and W-9 exactly.

### Bank Account Letters and Voided Checks

Some payors require a bank account verification letter; others ask for a voided check. If the name on your bank account does not match your CP-575 and W-9, payors may reject payments, causing delays in reimbursement.

### The Matching Rule: Treat It Like a Password

Payors expect every document — CP-575, W-9, NPI, contracts, bank letters, and more — to match perfectly. "ABC Healthcare, LLC" vs. "ABC Healthcare LLC" (without the comma) can cause major issues. One small typo, and the system won't let you through.

Keeping enrollment active requires ongoing updates: Medicare requires **revalidation every few years** (if missed, enrollment can be deactivated); practice changes (new address) must be updated in PECOS; Medicare may request additional documentation.

### What Is PECOS Surrogacy Access?

**PECOS (Provider Enrollment, Chain, and Ownership System)** is Medicare's online enrollment system. Surrogacy access lets a provider delegate access to a trusted third party — allowing them to manage enrollments, submit updates, and handle compliance tasks without sharing your login credentials.

Without surrogacy access: only you or your practice staff can make enrollment changes; delays in updating PECOS can lead to billing issues or enrollment lapses.

With surrogacy access: your Medicare enrollment is managed behind the scenes; routine updates (like revalidations) are handled without your direct involvement; issues can be resolved immediately.

### Why This Step Exists

Medicare introduced PECOS Surrogacy Access to replace the insecure practice of providers sharing personal login credentials with office staff or credentialing teams.

### How to Set Up Surrogacy Access

1. Confirm your Authorized Official (AO) status
2. Log in to PECOS and navigate to "My Connections"
3. Search for your credentialing team's NPI (your specialist will provide this)
4. Grant access and select PECOS/NPPES permissions
5. Approve the request in the CMS I&A System
