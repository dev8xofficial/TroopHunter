I was an early Salesforce fan. I mean genuinely early. Back around 2000, they launched a bold "No Software" campaign at a time when the idea of running a CRM in a browser without installing anything felt like a miracle. I'm dating myself a little here, but I remember when that was a revelation.

Over the years I used Salesforce across several companies and industries: call center services, software companies, restaurant marketing, IT service management, insurance credentialing, PEOs. Each time, we started with the same energy. This is it. This is the system that's going to pull everything together.

And each time, we hit the same wall.

The moment you want Salesforce to work the way your business actually works, not the way a generic sales org works, you start bending. Custom objects, workflows, Visualforce pages, APIs, additional modules. Then comes the consultant conversation. Then the quote. Then the realization that what you thought was a platform was actually a construction project with no end date and an open budget.

It's not that Salesforce can't do what you need. It can do almost anything. That's part of the problem. "You can build anything" is a very different promise from "this works for your business." One of them takes six months and a certified partner. The other one should just work.

What I kept running into wasn't a bad product. We just spent more time making it fit than making it work.

I'm not alone in this. A startup called Atonom recently cancelled their $40,000 Salesforce contract and built their own CRM using an AI tool called Lovable. Their head of finance built a working prototype in three hours. Annual cost now: $1,200. Plenty of companies have built their entire business on Salesforce and will stay that way, and for good reason. The ecosystem is real, the integrations run deep, and they've earned their position. But this story isn't unusual anymore. It's becoming a pattern.

Surely, Salesforce sees it coming. Their answer is Agentforce, their AI agent platform, which is a genuine response to a real threat. I'm not pulling against them. I actually think they'll be fine for a long time.

But I do find myself asking a real question: does their size help them or hurt them here? Blockbuster had thousands of locations, loyal customers, and a real business when Netflix came along. The question was never whether Blockbuster was good. It was whether the weight of what they'd built made it impossible to move fast enough. Salesforce isn't Blockbuster. But the question feels worth asking. Will they have to get lean? Restructure the pricing model? Rethink what "enterprise software" even means when a non-engineer can build a working CRM in an afternoon?

I genuinely don't know. Nobody does. Innovation is moving faster than anyone's predictions right now, including mine. I'll be watching what Salesforce does next with a lot of curiosity and, honestly, some respect for the problem they're trying to solve.

Rory Sutherland (Vice Chairman at Ogilvy and author of Alchemy) tells a story that made a huge impression on me. (I'm a big fan of his work. He's hilarious, sharp, and has a way of explaining human behavior through the most unexpected examples: elevators, speedometers, train tickets, Red Bull cans, ketchup.)

In this case, people were complaining that the elevators in a building were too slow. The logical solutions were expensive: upgrade the motors, rebuild the shafts, re-engineer the whole system. Instead, someone put mirrors in the lobby. The elevators didn't get any faster, but people stopped minding the wait as much because their attention went somewhere else.

It's a brilliant example of applied psychology. The problem wasn't only the time; it was the experience of the time. (Behavioral health providers, this is your home turf.)

Insurance credentialing, the process of enrolling providers with health plans so they can see patients and get paid, has its own version of that problem.

In many situations, the credentialing team ends up holding the bag. They're the visible part of a process whose timing is largely controlled by payors. When things take too long, the messenger is the one who gets shot.

Assuming the credentialer hasn't made an actual mistake, the real driver of delay is payor processing time. That lag doesn't just create frustration; it prevents providers from seeing patients and practices from getting paid.

There's only so much anyone can do to accelerate that part of the process. A meaningful portion of the credentialing timeline sits outside the control of the practice, the provider, or the vendor helping them. But, just like the mirrors in the lobby, you can change the experience of that wait.

In a credentialing context, that looks less like magic and more like doing the basics with intent. It means setting clear expectations up front about how long things typically take and why. It means communicating regularly, even when the honest update is, "There's been no movement this week; here's exactly where the file is in the plan's process." It means giving practices and providers a way to see status and next steps, rather than leaving them to wonder if anyone is working on their file. And it means making it clear when a delay is due to payor processing rather than a credentialing error, so the blame doesn't automatically land on the person closest to the provider.

None of this makes the elevators go faster. But, it does make the waiting feel less... long?

The opportunity isn't pretending we can erase the wait everywhere. It's being much more deliberate about how transparent, predictable, and humane one can make that time for providers and practices.

If you run a practice, work as a provider, or handle credentialing yourself: what has actually made the credentialing wait more bearable, or less so, in your world?

What Do Michael Gerber and Clorox's Formula 409 Have to Do with Running a Business?

In The E-Myth, Michael Gerber draws a distinction between working in the business and working on the business.

It's a simple idea. It's not simple to live out.

Managers work in the business. They supervise execution. They make sure tasks are completed correctly and on time. They stay close to people, process, and output.

Leaders work on the business. They decide direction. They design systems. They determine what the machine should produce and how it should function.

When you start a company, you don't get to choose. You are both. You execute and you architect. You fix problems and you build structure.

Over time, if you pay attention, the role shifts.

You begin to see patterns. You recognize where results are inconsistent. You realize that pushing harder on people doesn't fix structural weaknesses.

Design does.

Right now I'm deep in process work. Deep in the details. Not to supervise every moving part forever, but to refine the system itself so it produces consistent results.

Clorox's Formula 409 didn't get its name because the first version worked. It reportedly took hundreds of iterations to get it right.

Building a business feels similar.

I'm not sure which version we're on. But I hope it's around 408.

My New Year's resolution is simple: keep my vision alive.

Everything I've built or tried to build started the same way. I'd see a process that felt harder than it needed to be. Too manual. Too confusing. Too slow. Too many steps that existed "because that's how it's always been." And I'd have a vision for how it could work better.

Henry Ford, founder of the Ford Motor Company, is often credited with saying that if he built what people wanted, he would've made faster horses instead of cars. I don't take that as an excuse to ignore users. I take it as a reminder that listening and leading are not the same thing.

A lot of my ideas have come from the outside. Not from being formally trained in a domain, but from being close enough to see friction and care about removing it. I've been motivated by the same thing each time: build something useful that makes a painful process feel simpler and more humane.

Of course, once you start, reality shows up fast. The people doing the day-to-day work have feedback. Strong feedback. If you're building something real, alignment with the core pain is everything. The product gets better because of it.

But I've also learned that if you aren't careful, feedback turns into design-by-committee. And slowly the original idea gets diluted until it's just a cleaner version of the old process instead of a better way forward.

So this year, I want to keep doing both: listen closely, learn fast, and keep improving what I'm building while protecting the original vision that made me start in the first place.

That's the resolution. Keep the vision alive.

Propel's recent article on rising PEO adoption highlights an important shift. More businesses are choosing PEOs because running a company has become more complex. HR, payroll, compliance, benefits, and risk management all require more structure than most small and midsize businesses can handle on their own.

In my experience, the PEOs that handle growth well share a few things in common. They take the time to map their full client lifecycle and truly understand how information flows through their organization. They look for friction points, unclear handoffs, duplicate work, and moments where teams lose visibility. They invest in tools and processes that support alignment, reduce repetitive tasks, and give internal teams the information they need when they need it.

These steps do not require a finished or polished platform. They simply require clarity about how the organization wants to operate as it grows.

- Onboarding becomes more predictable.
- Client expectations become easier to manage.
- Teams spend less time tracking down information.
- Service delivery becomes more consistent.
- Growth feels sustainable rather than overwhelming.

As more businesses turn to PEOs, the expectations placed on PEO teams will continue to rise. The service model itself is strong. The next step is making sure the underlying structure can support the demand ahead.

This is the direction the industry is moving toward, and it is where many PEOs are now choosing to spend their time and attention. The organizations that invest early in their systems and processes will be better positioned to deliver the level of clarity, speed, and reliability that clients are beginning to expect.

There's a pattern I've noticed in Q4, and it's consistent in the two areas where I spend the most time: PEO and HR operations, and healthcare and insurance credentialing.

In the PEO space, one of the biggest misunderstandings is that signing in January means everything begins in January. It rarely does. There is real work on the client's side that has to happen first. Data needs to be gathered, benefit choices need to be finalized, payroll history has to be reviewed, and the new processes need to be aligned. Even PEOs with strong platforms and well-run onboarding teams can't make up for the sheer amount of work required before the system can actually take over. PEOs are not the slowdown. The lift simply takes time, and when the decision comes later, the window for a smooth start is already narrow.

Healthcare is similar, but the stakes are financial from day one. Providers can't bill until they are enrolled, and an application can't move forward until the provider is hired, their documents are in order, and the information submitted is correct and consistent across systems. Even the most organized credentialing workflows can't make up for missing documents, outdated details, or errors that must be resolved before a payor will begin processing. If these steps don't start until January, revenue is pushed out far later than most practices expect.

I'm sure this pattern shows up in other fields too. If you've seen something similar in your world, I'd be interested to hear how it plays out for you.

My dad used to say the best time to plant a tree was twenty years ago and the second best time is today. That idea applies here. You still need to think things through and weigh your options, but waiting until the new year often shifts the entire rhythm of Q1.

Making the decision in December — or better yet, November — gives January a chance to start on time instead of starting behind.
