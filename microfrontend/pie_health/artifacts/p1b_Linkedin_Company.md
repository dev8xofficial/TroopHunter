In January, 12 of the largest health plans in the country became the owners of CAQH.

If you don't know what that means for your practice, you need to.

CAQH — the system that stores your credentialing data, your licenses, your attestations, your practice demographics — is now governed by UnitedHealth Group, Centene, Aetna, Elevance Health, Cigna, Humana, and a coalition of Blue Cross Blue Shield plans.

Not partners. Not sponsors. Owners.

Here's why this matters:

CAQH was built as a neutral industry utility. A shared infrastructure that providers and payers both relied on. That's no longer what it is. It is now a payer-governed data system — where the organizations that decide whether to credential you, list you in their directories, and pay your claims also control the platform that holds your data.

What changes in practice:
→ Payers are already using CAQH status as a live enforcement mechanism
→ Expired or incomplete profiles are being flagged faster than before
→ The gap between a profile lapse and a revenue disruption just got shorter
→ Directory removals are happening with less warning

Most providers missed this. It happened quietly in January and has received almost no coverage in provider-facing media.

Has your CAQH profile been updated since January? If not, that's your action item for today.

Log in at proview.caqh.org and check your attestation date. If it's been more than 120 days, you're already past due.

Prior Auths Are About to Get Stricter, Not Easier!

Picture this: your team submits a prior authorization for a new provider. The service is appropriate, the documentation is clean, and the patient is waiting. But the request never reaches a reviewer. It’s denied instantly because the provider isn’t fully active in the payor’s system yet. The visit is delayed, and the revenue is gone.

That scenario will become far more common on January 1, 2026.

CMS’s new rules require payors to move faster than ever:
 • 72 hours for expedited requests
 • 7 days for standard requests
 • Clear, standardized denial reasons
 • Public reporting by March 31

To meet these deadlines, payors will rely on automated checks. And the first check is simple:

Is the provider active in the network? If not, the prior auth stops immediately—long before anyone looks at medical necessity.

This affects any specialty that relies on authorization: imaging, PT/OT, procedures, behavioral health, medication management, and more.
In 2026, “credentialed” won’t be enough. Providers must be fully loaded, linked, and recognized in each plan’s system—or the authorization fails by default.

For practices onboarding clinicians or expanding services early in the year, this rule change makes tight coordination between scheduling and credentialing essential. Even short enrollment gaps will translate into denied care and delayed revenue once these rules take effect.

A lot of the practices we support are behavioral health groups, and the upcoming UnitedHealthcare and Optum credentialing changes will matter for them as we move into 2026.

Under UHC’s updated 2025–2027 credentialing plan, applications can’t be older than 180 days and primary-source verifications can’t be older than 120 days. If those windows close before the payor finishes its internal review, the file can be restarted — even when the practice submitted everything correctly.
It’s easy to see why that feels unfair. A delay on the payor’s side can still result in lost time on the provider’s side.

The only practical protection a practice has is to eliminate every avoidable reason for UnitedHealthcare or Optum to pause or re-verify an application. That means keeping CAQH fully attested, making sure all data matches across systems, and submitting complete, fully verified applications the moment a provider is licensed.

These steps don’t change the structure of the process, but they do prevent a slow review from turning into a full restart — which is where practices lose the most time and revenue.
