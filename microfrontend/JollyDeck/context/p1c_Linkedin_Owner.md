Remember the meltdown when Windows 7 launched in 2009? Windows XP (its predecessor) was familiar, comfortable — like that favourite t-shirt you refuse to throw in the bin. Microsoft got a lot of heat for releasing something so new as a replacement for something so entrenched as the old XP. Now, Google Podcasts is shutting down, and even though there are better options, I'm dragging my feet to switch.

So why do we cling to old software?

- Habit trap: We get used to how things work. A new platform, even a better one, means learning new stuff.
- Fear of the unknown: Switching things up can be scary. Who knows how complicated that new podcast app might be?
- Nostalgia: Sometimes, it's just about what you're used to. Google Podcasts wasn't fancy, but it was mine.

But here's the thing: people switch products all the time — new phones, cars, diets. So the key question might be: can we make switching to new software a painless, even great experience?

Why I don't like the phrase "Keep the human in the loop"?

Because it suggests that the roles of humans and AI are equal.

That's far from being true (at least for now).

In most of today's working processes, humans are still the ones who understand the broader context, who need to perform and are, essentially, accountable for the results of their work.

AI, on the other hand, is still only a (really useful and powerful) tool that helps us with our work.

That's why I prefer the view that puts the human at the centre. We're controlling the work, we're making the decisions, and we're accountable for our work.

And that's why I giggle every time I encounter an AI-based solution that's supposed to do the work instead of us.

We're not there yet. Feel free to convince me otherwise.

Lost in between the LLM wars and Canva's cringe rap performance, you might have missed a crucial research paper by Google: "Towards Responsible Development of Generative AI for Education: An Evaluation-Driven Approach."

This paper accompanied the release of a family of language models fine-tuned for learning, called LearnLM. The paper highlights the biggest flaw of current AI models for educational purposes: LLMs are trained to be "helpful" rather than explicitly optimised for teaching.

The paper's findings focus on LearnLM-Tutor (the fine-tuned model). It significantly outperformed Gemini 1.0 (the base model) by:
- Staying on topic better
- Guiding users towards the answer
- Promoting active engagement
- Adapting to the learner's level

However, LearnLM-Tutor is also perceived as less friendly and encouraging than Gemini 1.0.

Kudos to Google for focusing on two crucial shortcomings in education:
1. Lack of high quality training data
2. Lack of robust and reproducible assessment frameworks

Building learning applications on top of better foundation models will increase the impact of learning technologies in ways we couldn't have dreamt of a couple of years ago.
