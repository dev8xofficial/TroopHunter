Most veterans with service-connected insomnia are rated as part of their PTSD, depression, or anxiety claim - not as a standalone condition.

Here's the reality: insomnia by itself rarely gets you a VA rating. But when it's bundled with the mental health condition causing it, sleep problems become concrete evidence for a higher rating. The VA sees chronic sleep impairment as a supporting symptom that can push you from 20% to 30%, or 50% to 70%, depending on how badly it's affecting your life.

If you served in combat or deployments, your sleep got wrecked by hypervigilance and irregular schedules. If you came home with chronic pain, your knees or back keep you awake at night. If PTSD nightmares are the problem, that's all documented in the same mental health evaluation. The key is tying insomnia directly to something the VA already recognizes as service-connected.

The diagnostic code for primary sleep disorders is DC 6847, but the criteria there are mostly built for sleep apnea - which rates at 50% with a CPAP machine. Straight insomnia doesn't fit those brackets well. That's why your best move is claiming insomnia as a secondary condition or as a symptom supporting your main diagnosis. At minimum, chronic sleep impairment gets you to 30% when it's part of a mental health rating.

What kills most claims is vagueness. Saying "I don't sleep well" doesn't work. Saying "I fall asleep in 2-3 hours, wake 4-5 times per night, average 3-4 hours total, and can't focus at work" does work. Keep a sleep diary for two weeks before your C&P exam. Write down bedtime, time to fall asleep, number of awakenings, total sleep hours, and how you felt the next day. Bring that to your exam. The examiner will ask specific questions - answer with numbers, not generalizations.

Secondary conditions matter here too. If insomnia triggers depression, or chronic pain insomnia leads to anxiety, you can claim both. Sleep deprivation is a real medical mechanism that explains why secondary conditions develop. Your personal statement should spell this out: "My insomnia from PTSD causes daytime fatigue and irritability, which worsened my depression."

If your blood pressure was high during service, the VA might owe you money - even if medication controls it now.

Hypertension is rated at 10%, 20%, 40%, or 60% depending on your readings and medication needs. Most veterans get 10%, which requires documented medication use for control. The real work isn't the rating itself - it's proving service connection. You need either readings from your service records, a first diagnosis during your time in, or a secondary connection to something already rated like PTSD or sleep apnea.

Vietnam veterans got a significant win recently. Hypertension is now presumptively connected to Agent Orange exposure, meaning you don't have to prove the link if you served in country. If you were exposed to herbicides, file now. The VA recognizes this connection without you having to build a nexus statement.

Secondary claims often work better than direct claims. If you already have a rating for PTSD, sleep apnea, obesity, or diabetes, hypertension is frequently a documented result. Sleep apnea especially - there's strong medical evidence connecting the two. Get your VA doctor to document that your high blood pressure stems from a service-connected condition. That's often easier than finding old STRs showing elevated readings from 20 years ago.

At your C&P exam, don't stress about having high readings that day. If you're on medication, your BP might be controlled when you show up. That's fine - the examiner knows this. They're looking at your medication history, your typical readings, and whether you need continuous treatment for control. Bring records showing what your unmedicated readings looked like or what your normal range is. Avoid caffeine before the exam and get proper rest.

The claim timeline typically runs a few months. File on VA Form 21-526EZ and include your service records, current diagnosis, medications, and any medical evidence linking hypertension to service. A personal statement explaining when it started and how it affects you carries real weight. If you're pursuing a secondary claim, get a written statement from your VA doctor connecting it to your rated condition.

File this claim if you've got documented hypertension and any potential service connection - presumptive, direct, or secondary.

The VA will rate each hip separately, and most vets get 10-30% for movement limits or arthritis - but hip replacement starts at 30% minimum and jumps higher if pain or weakness sticks around.

Hip conditions fall under diagnostic codes 5250-5255, and the rating depends entirely on what movements your hip can't do anymore. The VA measures flexion - bending, extension, abduction, adduction, and rotation - basically every direction your hip should move. If you can't bend your hip past a certain point, that maps to a specific rating. A 0% rating means your flexion is still normal - over 45 degrees. At 10%, flexion is capped at 45 degrees. Drop to 30 degrees of flexion and you're at 20%. Keep limiting that range and the rating climbs to 40% at just 10 degrees of flexion. The measurements matter because they're objective - the examiner measures them, writes them down, and the rating follows the rule.

Hip replacement is its own beast under DC 5054. You automatically get 100% for 4 months after implantation, then it drops to a minimum of 30% after that period ends. But if residual pain, weakness, or severe limitation of motion shows up on your post-surgery exams, you can get rated higher - 50% for moderate residuals, 70% for marked residuals, or even 90% if you're dealing with painful motion or need crutches to move. This isn't a permanent 100% - it's temporary protection during the first months while you heal, then the VA reassesses based on what actually remains.

Secondary claims are where hip conditions win. If you have a service-connected back, knee, or ankle condition that changed how you walk or moved, that altered gait puts stress on your hips. The VA recognizes this connection all the time. Your personal statement needs to spell out the link clearly: "My back injury causes me to favor my left side, which created compensatory stress on my hip." Service connection evidence includes your service treatment records - showing the original injury, your current diagnosis - X-rays, MRI, orthopedic evaluation, and functional limitations - can't climb stairs, can't sit past a certain time, need a cane. The C&P examiner will measure your range of motion and ask where pain starts during movement, so be specific about what hurts and when.

Most vets with shoulder pain don't realize the VA rates your dominant arm higher than your non-dominant arm - typically one tier up at the same severity, which can mean roughly 10 percentage points more on your rating.

Shoulder conditions are one of the top VA disability claims, and the ratings run from 0% to 80% depending on severity and which shoulder is affected. Here's the part that matters: your dominant arm (major) gets rated higher than your non-dominant arm (minor) for the same injury. That means if you're right-handed and your right shoulder is torn up, you're typically looking at a rating about 10 percentage points higher than the same injury would get on your left side - sometimes more at the upper tiers. The VA has specific diagnostic codes for this, and each one has its own rating table.

The most common ratings land in the 10-20% range for simple limitation of motion, but rotator cuff tears, recurrent dislocations, and severe immobility can push you to 30%, 40%, even 50% or higher depending on the code. Here's what doesn't get talked about enough: painful motion counts. Even if your range of motion technically doesn't qualify for a rating on paper, you can get at least 10% if you have X-ray evidence of arthritis and documented painful motion during your exam. A lot of vets leave that on the table because they don't know to emphasize where the pain starts, not where they can somehow force their arm to go.

The C&P exam is where your rating gets decided, and most people screw it up by pushing through pain to look tough. Stop moving when it hurts. That's the number the examiner writes down. Tell them exactly where the pain begins, what you can't lift anymore, whether your shoulder feels unstable, and how bad your flare-ups get. Mention your dominant arm specifically so they don't miss that you qualify for the higher rating. If you describe a good day instead of your worst days, you've just handed them reason to rate you lower.

Documentation wins these claims. You need service records showing shoulder injuries or treatment while you were in, imaging that proves something is actually wrong, and current medical evidence connecting it to your service. A private orthopedic evaluation with a nexus statement carries serious weight. Your MOS matters too, especially if you carried heavy loads or did overhead work that was part of your job. Buddy statements about the physical demands you shared count more than you'd think.

Every scar from your service can generate a separate VA rating - and painful ones automatically get 10%.

Service members leave the military with scars most people never see. Some are from combat, others from surgeries or training mishaps. What matters to the VA isn't how you got the scar - it's whether it causes pain, instability, disfigurement, or limits how your body moves.

The VA rates scars under diagnostic codes 7800-7805. Each code targets a different type of scar. Head, face, and neck scars are rated separately from the rest of your body because disfigurement matters more in those locations. A painful scar anywhere gets 10% automatically, with no additional proof needed beyond documentation. That means five painful scars equals 30% disability right there.

Here's what changes your rating: size, depth, location, and characteristics. A deep scar on your leg that's 20 square inches gets 20%. The same scar at 72 square inches jumps to 30%, and 144 square inches or more hits 40%. Scars that are unstable - meaning they break down, bleed, or weep regularly - get their own ratings on top of everything else. You can stack ratings. A large painful scar that also limits your knee motion could be rated under multiple codes at once.

Service connection is straightforward most of the time. If you were treated for the injury in service, that's your proof. If the scar came from surgery to fix a service-connected condition, the scar is automatically service-connected. No additional injury needed. Surgical scars from orthopedic repairs, abdominal surgery, or any other procedure count fully.

At the C&P exam, the examiner will measure every scar and document whether it's deep or superficial. Bring photos with a ruler for scale. Tell them about every painful scar, even if it's small. Describe any scar that breaks open or feels unstable. If a scar limits how far you can bend your knee or move your shoulder, say it clearly. The more specific you are about what each scar actually does to your function and comfort, the higher your rating typically goes.

The key mistake is underreporting. You don't get credit for scars you don't mention. Document them all, describe the pain or instability honestly, and let the evidence speak.

Radiculopathy claims can jump from 10% to 80% depending on which nerve is affected - but most veterans have no idea how the VA actually rates this.

The nerve pain radiating from your spine to your arms or legs gets rated under diagnostic codes 8510-8730, with each limb rated separately. That means if you have radiculopathy in both legs, you're not getting one rating - you're getting two, which can stack your disability percentage significantly higher. The difference between a 10% rating and a 60% rating often comes down to one thing: whether you have documented nerve involvement on an EMG or nerve conduction study.

Here's what the VA actually looks for. An EMG is the gold standard. It shows the examiner exactly which nerve is compressed and how badly. Without it, you're asking them to connect the dots between your spine condition and your radiating symptoms, which takes a stronger nexus statement and better documentation of your daily struggles. A herniated disc that's just sitting in your medical records is different from a herniated disc that the VA can see is actually pinching a nerve root on an MRI.

The rating tables break down by severity and location. Lower extremity radiculopathy affecting the sciatic nerve goes from 10% mild all the way to 80% complete paralysis. Upper extremity is similar, but it matters whether it's your dominant arm or not - your dominant arm gets higher ratings at the same severity level. Most radiculopathy claims come in as secondary to a service-connected spine condition, which means you need both documentation that the spine condition exists and a clear explanation of how it's causing the nerve pain.

At the C&P exam, the examiner will test your sensation, strength, and reflexes in the affected limb. They'll ask you to perform specific tests like straight leg raises or Spurling's maneuver. This is where precision matters. Don't just say "my leg hurts." Say "I have sharp, shooting pain from my lower back down the back of my right leg to my foot, and I have numbness on the outside of my calf." Map it. Be specific about weakness - "I have trouble lifting my foot when I walk" - because that's the difference between mild and moderate severity.

If you were stationed near a nuclear test site or handled weapons-grade material, you don't need to prove radiation caused your cancer - the VA already does.

Veterans exposed to atomic or nuclear radiation during military service have a significant advantage: presumptive conditions. This means if you can prove you were exposed to radiation in a recognized military context and you've developed one of the VA-listed conditions, the VA presumes the exposure caused it. No nexus letter. No causation argument. Just proof of exposure plus diagnosis equals compensation. Processing typically takes a few months, and approval rates for properly documented presumptive claims run substantially higher than most other disability claim categories.

Military radiation exposure comes in several distinct forms. Atomic testing participants served at Nevada, Bikini, Eniwetok, or other recognized test sites during the atmospheric testing era. Nuclear weapons workers handled uranium, plutonium, or other materials at production facilities. Occupational radiation exposure includes submarine reactor operators and nuclear weapons storage facility workers. Veterans who served in the occupation forces of Hiroshima or Nagasaki between August 6, 1945 and July 1, 1946 are also presumed exposed. The critical factor is that your DD 214 or service records must clearly document service in one of these capacities or at one of these locations. Vague service records create delays.

The VA recognizes a substantial list of presumptive conditions tied to radiation exposure. Cancers include thyroid, leukemia, lung, breast, pharyngeal, esophageal, stomach, pancreatic, bile duct, bladder, bone, and salivary gland cancers. The presumptive list under 38 CFR 3.309 is malignant-only - every recognized condition is a cancer or leukemia. Non-cancerous conditions you suspect are radiation-related still need a nexus letter linking your exposure to the diagnosis. The VA periodically updates the list, so verify current conditions at VA.gov before filing. If your diagnosed condition appears on that list and you have documented radiation exposure, you meet the presumptive standard. Diagnosis timing doesn't matter - radiation-linked conditions often develop many years after exposure.

If you haven't filed for PTSD yet, you're leaving money on the table - and it's not just about the check.

The VA rates PTSD on a scale from 0% to 100%, and combat veterans with documented deployments regularly get 50% or higher. But here's what most veterans don't know: the VA presumes your stressor is credible if you have a DD-214 showing combat service in a recognized zone. You don't have to prove the specific firefight or IED happened. They already believe you. Non-combat stressors like Military Sexual Trauma are different - those require corroborating evidence, but the VA has built-in markers - transfer requests, behavioral changes, medical records designed to help you prove it happened even if you don't have an incident report.

The real barrier isn't credibility. It's documentation of how PTSD actually impacts your life right now. The rating criteria focus on occupational and social impairment - can you work? Can you maintain relationships? Do you sleep? The VA looks at whether you have a current diagnosis from a mental health provider - VA or private, and then they measure the severity. A 50% rating means panic attacks more than once a week, memory problems, mood disturbances, and difficulty keeping work relationships stable. A 70% requires deficiencies in most areas: work, family, judgment, thinking. A 100% means you can't function independently - suicidal ideation, near-constant panic, inability to do basic self-care. Combat PTSD claims take a few months on average, but the speed depends on how organized your evidence is.

Your C&P exam is where the rating decision gets made. The examiner will ask about your stressor event, your symptoms now, how sleep is, whether you've lost jobs, how relationships are. Write it down before you go in. Don't minimize. If you have nightmares three times a week, say that. If you've had three jobs in five years because of irritability, that matters. Bring a buddy or spouse if the VA allows it - they can validate what they've seen. If the examiner seems dismissive or rushes through, you can request a second opinion or appeal later.

My tinnitus post this week passed 8,000 views, and the comments were a masterclass in how much bad information veterans get handed about their claims.

Three myths showed up over and over. Here's what's actually true.

Myth 1: There's a list of MOS or AFSC codes that gets you an automatic 10% for tinnitus.

Reality: No automatic list exists. What's real is the VA's Duty MOS Noise Exposure Listing, which concedes that certain jobs were exposed to hazardous noise. That clears the exposure hurdle for you, which is big - but you still need a current diagnosis and a C&P exam. It feels automatic because tinnitus is subjective and capped at 10%, so once exposure is conceded it's often granted. Often is not automatic.

Myth 2: You can rate hearing loss, tinnitus, and vertigo together under Meniere's for a higher number, with or without a diagnosis.

Reality: You can only use the Meniere's code, DC 6205, if you actually carry a Meniere's diagnosis. No diagnosis, no 6205. Myth 3: If you have ringing in both ears, you can get 20%.

Reality: Tinnitus is a flat 10% under DC 6260 - one rating, whether you hear it in one ear, both ears, or in your head. There is no 20% for tinnitus. The path to more compensation isn't a higher tinnitus rating - it's the secondary conditions tinnitus is often linked to, like anxiety, depression, or sleep problems.

The through-line in all three: the system rewards a clean diagnosis, a clear nexus, and a well-documented exam - not shortcuts. And an accredited VSO will help you build exactly that for free.

If you've had a C&P exam for hearing loss or tinnitus, you know the audiologist is basically reading your ears like they're a military manual.

The C&P exam for hearing conditions isn't just about how loud things need to be before you hear them. It's about a full picture: objective audiometric testing - the soundproof booth, the tones, the numbers, plus what your hearing loss actually costs you in daily life. The exam typically runs 60 to 90 minutes and covers your service noise exposure, baseline hearing before military service, current hearing patterns, tinnitus characteristics if you have it, and real-world functional impact on work and relationships. An audiologist or ENT specialist runs the whole thing.

What strengthens a hearing claim is specific detail about your service. Don't say "general military service." Say "infantry with daily weapons fire," "20 years in helicopters," or "mechanized unit with constant vehicle noise." Examiners need to trace the line from what you were exposed to, when you were exposed, and whether you actually had hearing protection available. They'll also ask if you have baseline hearing tests from military entrance exams or periodic tests during service. Even if you don't remember taking them, military medical records with those baseline tests are powerful because they prove your hearing was normal before the damage happened.

The objective testing is straightforward: you sit in a soundproof booth, tones play at different frequencies, you signal when you hear them, and the results get plotted in decibels of hearing loss. Speech discrimination testing shows how well you understand words despite the loss. Tympanometry checks eardrum function. This is the data the VA weights heavily for your rating. One key thing: respond honestly during testing, not when you're guessing. Missing tones is better than false positives because the accuracy matters.

Tinnitus gets its own assessment. Be specific about what you hear - high-pitched ringing versus low buzzing, whether it's constant or comes and goes, and what it costs you - sleep disruption, concentration problems, work impact. Same with hearing loss functional impact: don't just say it's hard. Say "I can't hear my wife if there's background noise," "I missed promotions due to communication difficulties," or "I need email at work instead of phone calls." Examiners want the actual scenario, not the summary.

If PTSD has you hearing constant ringing, that's not something you just live with - it's a secondary condition the VA can compensate.

PTSD doesn't just affect your mind. It can change how your nervous system processes sound. Hypervigilance keeps your brain on alert, which can make you more sensitive to everything - including tinnitus that might otherwise fade into the background. Poor sleep from PTSD nightmares can reduce your brain's ability to filter background sound, so the ringing feels louder and more intrusive. This is a recognized connection, and the VA does grant tinnitus as secondary to PTSD.

Here's what most veterans miss: you don't need blast exposure or noise damage to claim tinnitus. If your PTSD is already service-connected, you can file tinnitus as a secondary condition based on how PTSD symptoms trigger or worsen the ringing. One thing to set expectations honestly: tinnitus is capped at 10% under the VA's rating schedule, and the VA uses combined-rating math, not simple addition. So a 10% tinnitus rating may or may not raise your monthly check - for some veterans it bumps them into the next bracket, for others the rounding absorbs it. It's still worth claiming, because it builds your record and can matter for future increases.

The key is establishing the connection with medical evidence. You need current psychiatric records showing both your PTSD and tinnitus, treatment notes that mention how your anxiety or sleep disruption affects the ringing, and ideally an audiogram confirming tinnitus is present. But the strongest piece is a nexus letter from a mental health provider treating your PTSD - someone who can write clearly that your tinnitus is at least as likely as not caused or worsened by your service-connected PTSD. That letter is often what separates approvals from denials. Get it from the VA psychiatrist treating you if possible, or from a private provider who specializes in PTSD.

When you go to your C&P exam, don't just describe the ringing. Connect it to your PTSD symptoms. Tell the examiner how your nightmares wake you and make the tinnitus louder, how hypervigilance makes you more sensitive to the sound, how anxiety episodes trigger spikes in the ringing. Give concrete examples. The examiner needs to see this isn't isolated tinnitus - it's tinnitus tied to your service-connected PTSD.

File this claim if you have service-connected PTSD and experience tinnitus. The connection is real, the evidence is documentable, and it's worth pursuing.

If you think peripheral neuropathy only rates at 10%, you're leaving serious money on the table - especially if it's in multiple limbs.

The VA rates peripheral neuropathy per affected nerve and per limb. That means a veteran with diabetic neuropathy in both feet and both hands could rack up multiple ratings that combine into something substantial. A moderate sciatic nerve injury in each leg is 20% per leg. Add mild ulnar nerve damage in both arms at 10% each, and you're looking at combined ratings well above what most veterans expect going in.

The evidence piece matters more than people realize. EMG and nerve conduction studies are the gold standard - that's what the VA wants to see. Without them, your claim sits in weak territory. If you've got diabetes that's already service-connected, secondary service connection for neuropathy is usually a straight line. Your doctor just needs to document that the neuropathy stems from the diabetes. The harder path is proving direct service connection, which requires either in-service nerve injury, toxic exposure during service, or cold injury residuals with continuous symptoms since discharge.

Agent Orange exposure changes everything if you served in Vietnam, the Korean DMZ, or Thailand bases between specific date ranges. Without a nexus letter, the VA will deny a service-connection claim even when the link between your condition and your service is medically obvious.

A nexus letter is a written medical opinion that connects your current condition to your military service. The VA requires it for nearly every service-connection claim that isn't a presumptive condition (Agent Orange, Camp Lejeune, PACT Act lists, etc.). Without it the VA looks at your file, sees no doctor saying "this came from service," and denies the claim - even when the connection is obvious to you.

What makes a nexus letter work in VA terms is a medical opinion stated with "at least as likely as not" certainty - 50% or greater probability that your condition is connected to a documented in-service event, injury, or exposure. The doctor must explain the medical reasoning, not just state the conclusion. "This is service-connected" with no rationale gets dismissed.

The strongest letters cover four things:
- Records review: The doctor confirms they reviewed your service treatment records, VA records, and current diagnosis.
- The link: A specific in-service event, exposure, or injury tied to the current condition.
- Medical mechanism: How the in-service event physically causes the current condition (e.g., "blast exposure damages vestibular structures, producing the chronic dizziness documented here").
- Certainty language: Stated in VA terms: "at least as likely as not" or "more likely than not."

Who can write one matters. Your treating VA doctor is ideal because they know your records. Private specialists (orthopedist, psychiatrist, neurologist, audiologist) work too if your condition is in their lane. General practitioners can write them but carry less weight on complex cases. Independent Medical Examination (IME) services connect you with VA-experienced doctors but cost money - useful when no treating doctor will write one.

Before paying for an IME ask three things: Is the doctor board-certified in the relevant specialty? Will they review YOUR records or work from a template? Will they show you a sample letter? Anyone refusing those answers is a red flag.

Most service members have no idea they're keeping money that could cover a car payment - and that's exactly how BAH works.

BAH is tax-free income designed to cover civilian housing costs at your duty station. Here's the part that matters: you don't get reimbursed for actual rent. If your BAH rate is $2,500 and you rent a place for $2,200, you keep the $300. If you spend $2,800, you pay the overage yourself. That's the core mechanic - BAH is an allowance, not a reimbursement.

The Defense Travel Management Office sets BAH rates annually based on median rental market surveys at each duty location. Higher-ranking service members get more BAH than junior enlisted in the same location because housing expectations scale with rank. A dependents/no-dependents split determines the final rate - one extra dependent doesn't increase your BAH, but gaining your first dependent does.

Your BAH rate hinges on three things: your permanent duty station ZIP code (not where you actually live), your pay grade, and whether you have dependents. San Diego BAH is meaningfully higher than Fort Leonard Wood BAH because the local rental market is more expensive. If rates drop in a survey year and your BAH decreases, you're rate-protected - you keep your old rate until you PCS or your dependency status changes.

OCONUS service members receive OHA instead of BAH, and OHA is a reimbursement system tied to your actual lease amount up to a cap - you don't keep any difference. When you separate, BAH stops on your separation date with no transition benefit. BAH is part of your real compensation - treat it like the income it is when you're making transition decisions.

Then layer in other resources - Boots to Business for training, VBOC for mentoring, SBA loan programs for capital. No single program covers the whole journey, but combined they give you real options.

Know what your benefits actually do so you don't waste them and don't break the rules.

If you're a military retiree with a combat-related disability, you might be leaving thousands on the table by not knowing about CRSC.

CRSC (Combat-Related Special Compensation) is a Department of Defense program that restores (tax-free) the portion of your retirement pay you'd otherwise have to waive to collect VA disability, for the portion attributable to combat-related disabilities. For decades, the military subtracted your VA pay from your retirement. CRSC eliminated that for combat-related disabilities. But here's the catch: it's not automatic, and the combat-related standard is stricter than you might think.

To qualify, you need three things: a military retirement (20+ years, disability retirement, or Reserve pay), a VA rating of at least 10% for combat-related conditions, and proof that your disability connects to actual combat, hazardous service (like parachuting or diving), military equipment, or combat training exercises. A back injury from heavy lifting on base or hearing loss from mechanical maintenance won't qualify - even if the VA rates them. The distinction matters because CRSC calculates your payment based only on your combat-related conditions, not your full VA rating.

Here's a concrete example. Say you retired with $2,400/month gross pay and have a 70% total VA rating: 50% for combat-related PTSD and 20% for hearing loss from maintenance work. CRSC pays based on the 50% rating only, not the full 70%. Your monthly CRSC payment would be roughly $1,102 (the VA compensation rate for 50%), capped at your retirement pay ceiling. That's the money the military would have withheld before CRSC existed.

A major advantage most people overlook: CRSC is tax-free. Unlike CRDP, CRSC counts as disability compensation under federal tax code. If you're in the 22% bracket and CRSC pays $1,100 monthly, that's worth $1,410 in taxable income.

Critical to understand: you cannot draw both CRSC and CRDP at the same time. If you qualify for both, DFAS automatically pays the higher amount each month, and you can switch lanes during the annual open season in January if circumstances change.
