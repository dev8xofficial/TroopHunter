Debugging isn't going anywhere.

A friend asked me to look at a bug on their website. Mmap boundaries wouldn't respond to hover on page load—but after clicking or zooming, everything worked fine.

Classic race condition. The hover listeners were attaching before the map layers finished loading. User interaction triggered a re-render that accidentally "fixed" it.

This is a 10-minute fix once you understand why it's happening. But getting to that "why" requires knowing how map libraries initialize, how event listeners bind, and how async rendering actually works under the hood.

Here's the thing: AI can write map components all day. It can scaffold a Mapbox integration faster than I can type the import statement. But when the code almost works? When it fails silently on first load but mysteriously fixes itself? 

That's still a human problem.

Debugging is pattern recognition built on experience. It's knowing that "works after interaction but not on load" almost always means a timing issue. It's having seen enough weird edge cases that you develop intuition for where to look.

The more code AI writes, the more we need developers who can figure out why it's broken.

Learn to debug! 

It's not the flashy skill, but it's the one that actually ships products.

Last Cyber Monday, Shopify processed $7.9 BILLION in sales 🤯 That's 61 million people shopping simultaneously across 2+ million stores.

Zero downtime. Zero crashes.

📊 The Numbers:
Peak: 3.5 million requests per minute
44,000 orders per minute at peak
11,900 checkouts per second
Average page load: Under 2 seconds

🏗️ Their Architecture (Simplified):

1. Aggressive Caching 💾
They cache EVERYTHING that doesn't change per-user:
Product pages cached at CDN (Fastly)
95%+ of traffic never hits their servers
Only checkout/cart operations hit the database
The takeaway: Most e-commerce traffic is browsing, not buying. Cache product catalogs, images, and static content at the CDN level. 

2. Database Sharding 🗄️
Each store gets its own database shard. One store's traffic spike doesn't affect others.
This is brilliant isolation - a viral TikTok driving 100K visitors to one store doesn't slow down the other 2 million stores.
The takeaway: You probably don't need sharding yet, BUT the principle applies:
Use read replicas for heavy queries
Separate databases for analytics vs. transactions
Implement connection pooling to prevent connection exhaustion

3. Queue Everything ⏭️
Non-critical tasks go to queues:
Email confirmations: Queued ✉️
Analytics events: Queued 📊
Inventory sync: Queued 📦
Payment processing: Immediate 💳
Only revenue-critical operations happen synchronously.
The takeaway: Your checkout process shouldn't wait for email sending, analytics tracking, or data warehouse updates. Process the payment, show success, queue everything else.

4. Gradual Rollouts 🎯
They don't flip switches on Black Friday. New features deploy weeks early at 1% → 10% → 50% → 100%.
By the time Cyber Monday hits, their code has been battle-tested on millions of real transactions.
The takeaway: Feature flags aren't just for enterprise. Deploy early, test with small traffic percentages, catch issues before they're catastrophic.

5. Monitoring Everything 📈
They track:
Response times per endpoint
Database query performance
Error rates by store
Third-party API latency (payment processors, shipping APIs)
Alert when ANY metric crosses threshold.

The takeaway: You can't fix what you don't measure

🎯 The Shopify Approach That Surprised Me:
They focus on predictability over perfection.
They don't optimize everything. They optimize:
Payment processing (revenue) 💰
Cart operations (conversion) 🛒
Product page loads (discovery) 🔍
Everything else? Good enough is fine.
Email confirmation took 5 seconds instead of 1 second? Nobody cares. Cart took 5 seconds? You just lost the sale.

Your Cyber Monday Checklist: 📋
□ CDN configured for static assets 
□ Database connection pooling enabled
□ Background job queue set up 
□ Monitoring & alerts configured 
□ Load tested at 10x expected traffic 
□ Payment processor limits confirmed 
□ Rollback plan documented
□ Team knows who's on-call (if you have one) 
□ Status page ready 
□ Coffee ready ☕

When did we forget that "viable" is part of MVP?

Red flags your project is over-engineered:

🚩 You have 3+ repos for a pre-revenue product 
🚩 Your infrastructure costs more than your domain name 
🚩 You need a separate DevOps person before you have customers 
🚩 Your deployment process has more steps than your user flow 
🚩 You're running Kubernetes for traffic that could fit on a Raspberry Pi

When complexity IS justified:

✅ You're handling sensitive regulated data (healthcare, finance) 
✅ You have proven scale needs (100K+ monthly active users)
✅ You need multi-region redundancy for uptime SLAs 
✅ Your business model requires complex microservices (rare) 
✅ You're building platform infrastructure, not a product

The real cost of over-engineering is not just money, it's:

Slower iteration when you're learning what customers want
Harder to pivot when the market tells you something
Burning runway on infrastructure instead of features
Delayed launch while you're "perfecting" the architecture
I've seen startups run out of cash before they even launched because they spent 8 months building "scalable infrastructure" for users they hadn't acquired yet.

My approach for new projects:

Start with the simplest stack that works:
Serverless when possible (Vercel, Netlify, Firebase)
Managed services over self-hosted (Supabase over your own Postgres)
Monorepo until you have a concrete reason to split
Pay-as-you-go over fixed infrastructure costs
