What should clients do if they want everything (and more) on their website right away?
I’m constantly talking to business owners who want to either update their websites, build one from scratch, or migrate from a website builder to a custom site (often, when clients have run into limitations with a free patform, they no longer face those restrictions on a custom site and want to have everything on their website).

Businesses spend huge amounts of money trying to improve the product, while slowly making it less flexible, slower and harder to maintain.
So, perfectionism can quietly kill momentum.

I have countless real-world examples where clients, in their pursuit of perfection, have STILL not launched their website. But it could have been gradually developed over the years, been in use by the marketing team, had users, and been generating revenue. 
And that’s actually very sad. Because we live in a very fast-paced world now, and what clients are trying to make perfect today will be irrelevant a year from now. 

So, another thing I often see: businesses try to perfect every tiny detail before launch.
Every button has to be “just right.” Every animation has to feel perfect. Every scenario has to be covered immediately.
And this usually leads to one thing: delays.
As a client, you have every right to disagree with me: “I don’t want to redo it later.”
But what I say to my clients - You will redo parts of the system later anyway. Technology changes. User behavior changes. Business goals change. Market expectations change.

Modern digital products are NEVER truly stay finish. 

And trying to make everything perfect from day one often slows the project down more than it helps.
At Galaxy IT Inc., we usually work in sprints with staged releases. And it a one of the standard practice in development. 
And honestly, this approach works much better long-term.
Then improvements happen in the next sprints - based on actual usage, not assumptions.

Over the years I’ve spent managing development, I’ve noticed a pattern. 
My best projects the ones that took off, scaled exponentially, and generated the most revenue for clients have always been stories of trust. 
Stories where the client listened to me as the CTO or to my team.
It’s not that we’re the smartest, absolutelly no. It’s about the right division of roles.

You as a business owner is the expert in your niche. You know logistics, sales, customer needs etc. 
But when you try to design the technical architecture on their own or dictate to developers which databases to choose and which framework to use, the project usually starts to fall apart.

The best synergy happens when the CTO acts as a bridge between the business idea and the tech stack.

The client should say: “I need the user to be able to do this in two clicks, and the warehouse to receive a notification.” That’s it. That’s their job. As for how to implement it under the hood whether through custom APIs, or the right structure in Laravel that’s the work of the technical architect and the development team.

When my expertise is heeded, we don’t just write code based on the brief. We highlight hidden risks:
- where the system will crash under load,
- where the client is trying to order an expensive and unnecessary feature,
- or where, conversely, it’s worth investing in database security now so as not to lose everything in a year
- which server is best to choose and what factors to consider

That’s why you need to turn to a team of developers - for the expertise that will strengthen your business.
And when a business trusts the team’s technical expertise rather than trying to micromanage the code, the result is an enterprise-grade product.

Why 100% compliance with your Scope of Work (SOW) is the fastest way to lose money. 📉

When you hire an IT team, you expect them to build exactly "what’s on the paper." Most agencies do just that. Some are better than others, some hit deadlines, some don't. But if you come with a fixed request and the team follows it blindly, you’re in trouble.

Logic says: The SOW is the roadmap; the result is the destination.

But in complex software engineering, a "docile" developer is your biggest financial risk. Why? Because your business challenge is always deeper than a technical description.

Let’s look at a real-world scenario:
Imagine it’s Black Friday. You have 1,000 customers hitting your site simultaneously. Your SOW says, "System sends a confirmation email upon purchase."

If your team builds that "to the letter" without considering high-load architecture, your server will choke. Or worse, your email provider flags you for spam activity and shuts you down.

The financial fallout for your business:
Emails fail ➡️ Customers panic and flood support ➡️ Your ad spend is flushed down the drain ➡️ You pay double for emergency fixes on a system that was "already finished."

That’s why my team never aims for "100% compliance."

We aim for 120%. We look for the critical failure points that live outside your technical brief. You know your business; we know how to ensure the technology empowers it instead of bottlenecking it.

For any SMB, your software should be a profit-protecting asset, not a budget-draining liability.

I believe a true tech partner isn't someone who tells you what you want to hear—it’s someone who tells you what you need to hear to keep your business resilient.

Have you ever received "clean code" that resulted in a broken business process? Let’s talk in the comments. 👇

I will never recommend a jet engine when an EV is more than enough. I truly believe that knowing where to draw that line is a CTO’s most important responsibility.

Here is my roadmap for building cost-effective tech assets:

Scale horizontally, not "just in case".
You don’t need an infrastructure built for a million users on day one. Modern stacks (like Laravel) allow you to build systems that grow with you. Add power only when your sales actually demand it. Don’t pay for capacity you aren't using.

Choose "Predictability" over "Exotic Tech".

Invest in Test Automation, not "Headcount Bloat."
Here’s a pro-tip: instead of hiring three managers to manually check orders, invest once in automated tests for your critical workflows. It’s your insurance policy against a broken checkout page after a routine update.

You aren’t buying technology!
you’re buying a tool to capitalize your business. My responsibility is to ensure that every dollar you invest in that tool delivers maximum efficiency and zero waste.
